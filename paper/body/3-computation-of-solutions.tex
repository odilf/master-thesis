\chapter[Numerical approximation to Euler-Lagrange solutions]{Numerical approximation to\protect\\ Euler-Lagrange solutions}\label{sec:euler-lagrange-solutions}

Perhaps the most straightforward approach to numerically compute solutions that extremize the action given a Lagrangian is to compute the ODE that results from Euler-Lagrange~\eqref{eq:euler-lagrange}, and then use some numerical ODE computation scheme based on the underlying type of manifold. For example, if $Q \cong \RR^n$ then we can use Runge-Kutta, or if $Q$ is a Lie group we could use Runge-Kutta-Kaas~\cite{munthe-kaasRungeKuttaMethodsLie1998} or similar methods.

However, discretization of the resulting ODE based on $q(t)$ can have undesirable effects due to the loss of the underlying geometric picture. In \cref{sec:geometric-properties-el} we explain what these properties are and in \cref{sec:pitfalls-discrete-el-ode} we discuss what is lost when one stops working geometrically.

Instead, one can discretize the tangent bundle of the configuration space $TQ$ as $Q \times Q$, and derive discrete analogs of the Euler-Lagrange equations themselves. This approach, called \newterm{variational integration}~\cite[\S1.3]{marsdenDiscreteMechanicsVariational2001}, will be explained in \cref{sec:variational-integration}.

\section{Geometric properties of the Euler-Lagrange equations}\label{sec:geometric-properties-el}

The Euler-Lagrange equations can be viewed as a dynamical system on the \newterm{phase space} $TQ$. Given a Lagrangian $L: TQ \to \RR$, the \newterm{Lagrangian vector field} $X_L: TQ \to T(TQ)$ is the second-order vector field whose integral curves (i.e., curves whose velocity matches $X_L$ at every point) are the solutions of the Euler-Lagrange equation~\eqref{eq:euler-lagrange}. This has an associated flow, called the \newterm{EL (Euler-Lagrange) flow},
\[ F_t : TQ \to TQ, \quad (q(0), \dot{q}(0)) \mapsto (q(t), \dot{q}(t)), \]
that sends each initial state to the state reached after time $t$ along the corresponding solution. In other words, $F_t$ packages the physical evolution of \emph{every} initial condition simultaneously, rather than one trajectory at a time.

It is this flow that has two distinct sources of exactly conserved quantities, with different origins. The first, Noether's theorem, associates a conserved scalar to each continuous symmetry of $L$. The second, symplecticity, is a property of the variational structure of (unforced) Lagrangians, which in particular implies the exact preservation of a Hamiltonian function. Both of these constrain long-term behavior in ways that local error estimates often fail to capture. We will briefly explain the implications of each (for an in-depth examination, see~\cite[\S1.2]{marsdenDiscreteMechanicsVariational2001} and~\cite[\S\S7,~11]{marsdenIntroductionMechanicsSymmetry1999}).


\paragraph{Conservation laws and Noether's theorem}

Suppose $L$ admits a \newterm{continuous symmetry}: a family of diffeomorphisms $\Phi_s : Q \to Q$, with $\Phi_0 = \mathrm{id}$, that leaves $L$ invariant in the sense that
\begin{equation}
	L\left(\Phi_s(q),\; T\Phi_s \cdot \dot q\right) = L(q, \dot q) \qquad \text{for all } s, q, \dot q,
	\label{eq:symmetry-condition}
\end{equation}
These diffeomorphisms are generated infinitesimally by a vector field $X$ on $Q$ given by
\[
	X(q) = \odv{}{s}_{s=0} \Phi_s(q).
\]
\newterm{Noether's theorem} states that, in coordinates, the scalar function on $TQ$
\begin{equation}
	J(q, \dot q) = \sum_i \pdv{L}{\dot q^i} X^i(q)
	\label{eq:noether-charge}
\end{equation}
is conserved along every solution of the Euler-Lagrange equations~\cite[\S7]{marsdenIntroductionMechanicsSymmetry1999}. That is, $\odv{J}{t} = 0$.%\footnote{When the symmetries form a Lie group $G$, the conserved quantity can be written in a coordinate-free form using the Lagrangian symplectic form, called the \newterm{momentum map} $\odif*{} \langle J, \xi \rangle = \iota_{\xi_{TQ}} \omega_L$, which is the usual way Noether's theorem is expressed \tc{} \todo{Igual esto sobra}.}.

Well-known conservation laws can be derived in this way: translational symmetry of $L$ corresponds to linear momentum, rotational symmetry to angular momentum, time-translation symmetry (considering time as part of the state space, $Q \times \RR$) to energy, etc.




\paragraph{Symplecticity}

In the Euler-Lagrange derivation, integration by parts~\eqref{eq:el-parts} leaves a boundary term that ultimately evaluates to $0$ since the endpoints are fixed. However, we can consider this term on its own. Specifically, this term is a one-form on $TQ$, called the \newterm{Cartan one-form} $\theta_L$, expressed as $\pdv{L}{\dot{q}^i}\odif{q^i}$ in coordinates. Its exterior derivative
\[
	\omega_L = \odif*{} \theta_L
\]
is non-degenerate and closed, making it a symplectic form on $TQ$. The EL flow preserves this symplectic form $\omega_L$, in the sense that $F_t^* \omega_L = \omega_L$ for all $t$~\cite[\S1.2.3]{marsdenDiscreteMechanicsVariational2001}. This makes the EL flow \newterm{symplectic} (with respect to $\omega_L$).

Symplecticity of a flow implies that it is (locally) the flow of some Hamiltonian function $H$, and that that function is exactly preserved along the flow~\cite{marsdenIntroductionMechanicsSymmetry1999}. Additionally, symplectic flow also implies \newterm{Liouville's theorem}, which states that the EL flow preserves phase space volume. In particular, no region of phase space contracts or expands, so there are no attractors or sinks in the dynamics.

\section{Pitfalls of discretizing the equations of motion}\label{sec:pitfalls-discrete-el-ode}

Once we have written the Euler-Lagrange equation as a system of ODEs on $TQ$, the obvious move is to plug it into an off-the-shelf integrator (e.g., Runge-Kutta). While the local error per step is controlled, this approach loses the geometric structure and with it the geometric properties described in \cref{sec:geometric-properties-el}. A priori, a generic integrator does not preserve momentum maps nor is it symplectic. It is possible to construct, on a case-by-case basis, integrators tailored to conserve simple invariants such as linear or angular momentum~\cite[\S\S IV.1--IV.2]{hairerGeometricNumericalIntegration2006}, but it is hard to make this work for more complicated invariants~\cite[Thm.~IV.3.3]{hairerGeometricNumericalIntegration2006}. Symplectic integrators (that are not specific cases of the variational principle) are rarer still~\cite[\S\S VI.4.1,~VI.6.3;~Thm.~IV.6.4]{hairerGeometricNumericalIntegration2006}; and algorithms that exactly preserve energy cannot be symplectic for general (non-integrable) systems at fixed step size~\cite[\S3]{zhongLiePoissonHamiltonJacobiTheory1988}.


Furthermore, if $Q$ is not a vector space, sometimes the updates are done in a bigger ambient vector space that $Q$ can be embedded into. Then, accumulation of local errors can result in values that are not elements of $Q$ at all. For instance, with 3-dimensional rotations, integration of $\SO(3)$ matrices in the ambient matrix space $\RR^9$ often leads to loss of orthogonality and unit determinant. This can be mitigated to some extent by reprojecting onto the relevant manifold, but that might increase errors and makes convergence harder to analyze.

In short, naive integration produces long-term behavior that is qualitatively unphysical. This is especially problematic for conservation laws, as they might be assumed as invariants in a broader analytical context where a numerical integrator might be used.

\section{Variational integration}\label{sec:variational-integration}

It is possible to discretize the variational principle itself \cref{def:hamiltons-principle}, rather than the resulting equations of motion. The discrete flow then inherits, by construction, a discrete analog of each of the geometric properties of \cref{sec:geometric-properties-el}.

\bigskip

The discrete analog of a continuous path $q(t) : \RR \to TQ$ is a sequence of points in $Q$. The Lagrangian can be adapted to this discrete context into a \newterm{discrete Lagrangian} $L_d$ that acts on two consecutive points of a path (instead of one point in $TQ$):
\[ L: TQ \to \RR \quad \Rightarrow \quad L_d : Q \times Q \to \RR \]

Then, the discrete action is now composed of sums of these discrete Lagrangians. That is, for a path $\mathbf{q} = {\{ q_k \in Q \}}_{k=0}^N$, the discrete action $S_d$ is:

\begin{equation}
	S_d (\mathbf{q}) = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \Ld(q_k, q_{k+1})
	\label{eq:action-discrete}
\end{equation}

Notice that this is a reformulation that can be done with no loss of information. That is, an \newterm{exact discrete Lagrangian} $\Ld^e$ can be defined for a step size $h$ as
\begin{equation}
	L_d^e (q_0, q_1) = \int_0^h L(q_{0, 1}(t), \dot{q}_{0, 1}(t)) \odif{t}
\end{equation}
where $q_{0, 1}$ is the path that extremizes the action along all the paths that connect $q_0$ and $q_1$. This way the discrete formulation gives identical results to the continuous one. Of course, finding the path $q_{0,1}$ is as hard as the original continuous problem; so, in practice, the discrete Lagrangian $\Ld$ is defined using some quadrature rule that essentially approximates the integral of the exact discrete Lagrangian.

Applying Hamilton's principle to the discrete action (that is, $\mdif*{S_d} = 0$), the \newterm{discrete Euler-Lagrange} (DEL) equations can be derived:
\begin{equation}
	\mdif{2} L_d (q_{k-1}, q_k) + \mdif{1} L_d (q_k, q_{k+1}) = 0.
	\label{eq:euler-lagrange-discrete}
	\tag{DEL}
\end{equation}
Similarly to Hamilton's principle (\cref{def:hamiltons-principle}), the DEL equations underpin every result in this thesis.

These equations are well-defined for any manifold, since both $\mdif{2} L_d (q_{k-1}, q_k)$ and $\mdif{1} L_d (q_k, q_{k+1})$ live in $T^*_{q_k} Q$, which forms a vector space with well-defined addition.

\paragraph{Geometric properties of the DEL equations} As in the continuous case, the DEL equations~\eqref{eq:euler-lagrange-discrete} define a discrete flow $(q_{k-1}, q_k) \mapsto (q_k, q_{k+1})$ on $Q \times Q$. Using this discretization, the properties of \cref{sec:geometric-properties-el} are automatically inherited. The boundary term in the variation of the discrete action defines a discrete one-form\footnote{Technically, there are \emph{two} one-forms, $\theta^+$ and $\theta^-$, but both of these define the same symplectic two-form via the exterior derivative~\cite[p. 15]{marsdenDiscreteMechanicsVariational2001}.} on $Q \times Q$, whose exterior derivative is a symplectic form $\omega_{L_d}$. The discrete flow preserves $\omega_{L_d}$ exactly at every step. This makes the discrete flow symplectic, so it also exactly preserves some Hamiltonian $\widetilde H$. For the exact discrete Lagrangian $\Ld^e$, that Hamiltonian is the physical energy $H$; for a quadrature-based $L_d$ of order $p$, it approximates $H$ at order $O(h^p)$. This makes it so that the energy along the numerical trajectory doesn't oscillate further than $O(h^p)$, so while energy might not be 100\% conserved locally, the error is globally bounded. 

A discrete analog of Noether's theorem holds as well: any continuous symmetry of $L$ inherited by $L_d$ corresponds with a scalar quantity conserved exactly at every step. This is arguably stronger than the continuous Noether statement, since conservation now holds for the actual numerical trajectory rather than for an ideal one being approximated.


One symmetry that does not get automatically inherited is time-translation symmetry. The discrete Lagrangian $L_d(q_k, q_{k+1})$ implicitly depends on a fixed timestep $h$, and fixing $h$ removes the time variable from the discrete dynamics, so there is no variable left for time-translation to act on. This is why earlier we discussed energy oscillating around the true value instead of being preserved.

\bigskip

Given this approach to discretization, a trajectory can be computed point-by-point, computing $q_{k+1}$ from $q_k$ and $q_{k-1}$, given two initial datapoints. This is theoretically sound (assuming existence and uniqueness conditions) and a reasonable approach, amounting essentially to forward integration in time. However, if an endpoint is needed, this approach fundamentally does not work~\cite[p. 6]{ferraroParallelIterativeMethod2025}. The algorithm presented in the next section, which we study for the rest of the thesis, provides solutions to boundary value problems, and does so in parallel.

\section{Parallel algorithm}\label{sec:parallel-algorithm}

In~\cite{ferraroParallelIterativeMethod2025}, an iterative algorithm that can solve the discrete Euler-Lagrange equations is proposed. The idea of the parallel algorithm is to treat the (discrete) action as a function of which we want to find roots. I.e., given a path $\mathbf{q} = {\{q_k\}}_{k=0}^N \in Q^{N+1}$ and a discrete Lagrangian $\Ld$, we define its (global) residual $R$ as
\begin{equation}
	R(\mathbf{q}) = \bigoplus_{k=1}^{N-1}
	\left(\mdif{2} L_d (q_{k-1}, q_k) + \mdif{1} L_d (q_k, q_{k+1})\right)
	\label{eq:residual-global}
\end{equation}
(where $\bigoplus$ stacks the residuals into a vector in $\bigoplus_k T^*_{q_k}Q$), and we want to find a path $\mathbf{q}^*$ such that $R(\mathbf{q}^*) = 0$ where $q_0$ and $q_N$ are fixed.

At a high level, two schemes are used to find this root. First, we frame the algorithm as a block Jacobi update step that can be done in parallel. However, the Jacobi update is still not directly computable, so we use one or more Newton-Raphson steps to compute the Jacobi update.

\paragraph{Jacobi.}
The Jacobi update step consists of finding a new path $\overline{\mathbf{q}} = \{{ \overline{q}_k \}}_{k=0}^N$ that makes each summand of the residual $0$ with respect to the previous path. That is, we define the \newterm{local residual} as
\begin{equation}
	r_k(\mathbf{q}, \overline{q}) =
	\mdif{2} L_d (q_{k-1}, \overline{q}) + \mdif{1} L_d (\overline{q}, q_{k+1})
	\label{eq:residual-local}
\end{equation}
which is a term of the sum, replacing the central $q_k$ by some new value $\overline{q}$. An updated path $\overline{\mathbf{q}}$ where for all inner points $k$, $r_k(\mathbf{q}, \overline{q_k}) = 0$, is not generally a solution, but under certain conditions (discussed in \cref{sec:standard-convergence}), iterating this update will converge to a solution. Crucially, the local residual $r_k$ only involves $q_k$ and its two neighbors $q_{k-1}$ and $q_{k+1}$ (see \cref{fig:discrete-path-stencil}), so all interior points can be updated independently and in parallel.

\paragraph{Newton-Raphson.}
Since the Lagrangian can be arbitrarily complex, the Jacobi step does not have a general way to be computed. Specific kinds of Lagrangians, such as quadratic or cubic ones, have clear closed-form solutions. In lieu of knowing if the Lagrangian has one of these forms ahead of time, we use the Newton-Raphson method to solve a linearization of the problem. So, instead of considering the residual $r_k(\mathbf{q}, \overline{q})$, we consider the linearization $\rho_k$ around $q_k$:
\[
	\rho_k (\mathbf{q}, \overline{q}) = r_k(\mathbf{q}, q_k) + \mdif{2} r_k(\mathbf{q}, q_k) (\overline{q} - q_k)
\]
Note that this is the step where we apply the hypothesis that $Q$ is a vector space, since we are doing a linear combination based on the increment.

Finding the root is easy, amounting to solving the linear system
\begin{align*}
	r_k(\mathbf{q}, q_k) + \mdif{2} r_k(\mathbf{q}, q_k) (\overline{q} - q_k) & = 0                                                                                    \\
	\overline{q} - q_k                                                        & = \left(\mdif{2} r_k(\mathbf{q}, q_k)\right)^{-1} \left(-r_k(\mathbf{q}, q_k)\right)
\end{align*}
from which we get the general Jacobi-Newton update rule:
\begin{equation}
	\overline{q} = q_k - \left(\mdif{2} r_k(\mathbf{q}, q_k)\right)^{-1} r_k(\mathbf{q}, q_k).
	% \tag{Jacobi-Newton}
	\label{eq:jacobi-newton}
\end{equation}
In a standard discrete Lagrangian system, this derivative of the residual is given by the Hessian of the Lagrangian:
\begin{align*}
	\mdif{2} r_k (\mathbf{q}, q_k) & = \mdif{22} \Ld(q_{k-1}, q_k) + \mdif{11} \Ld(q_k, q_{k+1}) \\
	r_k (\mathbf{q}, q_k)          & = \mdif{2} \Ld(q_{k-1}, q_k) + \mdif{1} \Ld(q_k, q_{k+1})  
\end{align*}

It is equation~\eqref{eq:jacobi-newton} which is computable using any linear solver. The algorithm consists of repeating this step until we converge to a solution. Of course, the question now is to verify under what conditions this algorithm does, in fact, converge.

\subsection{Convergence criteria}\label{sec:standard-convergence}

The convergence of the method (both in the standard case as well in the forced and Lie group cases) is mainly related to the Hessian of the action, which we refer to as the \newterm{global Hessian matrix}, $H$. This is because, given a problem in the form $\nabla F = 0$, the Jacobi-Newton method converges locally if the Hessian of $F$ is positive definite~\cite[Prop. 4.5]{ferraroParallelIterativeMethod2025}. Hamilton's principle is precisely such a formulation: the residual that we want to eliminate is the gradient of the action, $R = \nabla S_d$.

Given the structure of the action, its Hessian can be written in terms of second-order derivatives of the Lagrangian. That is, given the action defined as in~\eqref{eq:action-discrete} for a path $\mathbf{q}$ of length $N$
\[ 	S_d (\mathbf{q}) : Q^{N+1} \to \RR, \qquad  S_d (\mathbf{q}) = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \Ld(q_k, q_{k+1})
\]
the derivative with respect to the $i$-th component is, as usual,
\begin{align*}
	\partial_{q_i} S_d (\mathbf{q}) & = \partial_{q_i} \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \Ld(q_k, q_{k+1})           \\
	                          & = \mdif{1} \Ld(q_i, q_{i+1}) + \mdif{2} \Ld(q_{i-1}, q_i)
\end{align*}
and then the entries of the Hessian are
\begin{align*}
	(H (\mathbf{q}))_{ij} = \pdv{S_d}!{q_i, q_j}(\mathbf{q})
	& = \pdv{\left(\mdif{1} \Ld(q_i, q_{i+1}) + \mdif{2} \Ld(q_{i-1}, q_i)\right)}!{q_j}          \\
	& = \delta_{i, j} \left( \mdif{11}{\Ld}(q_i, q_{i+1}) + \mdif{22}{\Ld}(q_{i - 1}, q_i) \right) \\
	& + \delta_{i+1,j} \mdif{12}{\Ld}(q_i, q_{i+1})                                               \\
	& + \delta_{i-1,j} \mdif{21}{\Ld}(q_{i-1}, q_i)
\end{align*}

This results in a tridiagonal matrix that can be written in the following form:
\begin{equation}
	H(\mathbf{q}) = \begin{pmatrix}
		\mathcal{D}_1       & \mathcal{C}_{1} &                         &                         &                     \\
		\mathcal{C}_{1}^{T} & \mathcal{D}_2   & \mathcal{C}_{2}         &                         &                     \\
		                    & \ddots          & \ddots                  & \ddots                  &                     \\
		                    &                 & \mathcal{C}_{N - 3}^{T} & \mathcal{D}_{N - 2}     & \mathcal{C}_{N - 2} \\
		                    &                 &                         & \mathcal{C}_{N - 2}^{T} & \mathcal{D}_{N - 1}
	\end{pmatrix}.
	\label{eq:hessian-matrix}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		\mathcal{D}_k & = \mdif{22} \Ld(q_{k-1}, q_k) + \mdif{11} \Ld(q_k, q_{k+1}) \\
		\mathcal{C}_k & = \mdif{12} \Ld(q_k, q_{k+1})
	\end{aligned}
	\label{eq:hessian-terms-1}
\end{equation}
where by Schwarz's theorem, $\mathcal{C}_k^T = \mdif{21} \Ld(q_k, q_{k+1})$.



Thus, the following theorem gives one of the fundamental criteria for convergence of the parallel algorithm (\cite[Prop. 4.3, 4.5, remark 4.6]{ferraroParallelIterativeMethod2025})
\begin{theorem}[Jacobi-Newton convergence criterion]\label{thm:hessian-pd-convergence}
	If the Hessian $H(\mathbf{q})$ is positive definite, there is a neighborhood $\mathcal{D}$ around a solution $\mathbf{q}^*$ such that any path $\mathbf{q} \in \mathcal{D}$ converges to $\mathbf{q}^*$.
\end{theorem}

This condition is sufficient, although in practice it is hard to compute, since it involves checking for positive-definiteness of an $(N-1)n \times (N-1)n$ matrix. The most common way to do this involves the Cholesky factorization, which has an $\mathcal{O}(N^3 n^3)$ runtime.

An easier-to-check condition can be given in terms of a \newterm{local Hessian matrix} $H_k$, which is obtained by decomposing the blocks of the global Hessian matrix as shown in \cref{fig:hessian-assembly}. The entries of the local Hessian matrix are the following:
\begin{equation}
	H_k(q_k, q_{k+1}) = \begin{pmatrix}
		\mathcal{A}_k   & \mathcal{C}_k \\
		\mathcal{C}_k^T & \mathcal{B}_k
	\end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		\mathcal{A}_k & = \mdif{11} \Ld(q_k, q_{k+1}) \\
		\mathcal{B}_k & = \mdif{22} \Ld(q_k, q_{k+1}) \\
	\end{aligned}
	\label{eq:hessian-terms-2}
\end{equation}
(note that $\mathcal{D}_k = \mathcal{B}_{k-1} + \mathcal{A}_k$).

\begin{theorem}[Local Hessian convergence~{\cite[Thm.~4.1]{ferraroParallelIterativeMethod2025}}]\label{thm:local-hessian-pd-convergence}
	If all the local Hessian matrices $H_k$ (as defined above) are positive definite, then the global Hessian matrix $H$ is positive definite and the method converges locally.
\end{theorem}

Checking this condition has an $\mathcal{O}(N n^3)$ runtime in terms of the size $N$ of the path and dimension $n$ of the configuration space. However, this condition is not necessary, and in practice it is common for p.d.\ global Hessians to not be locally p.d..

This is where a third convergence result comes into play, which gives an easy-to-check sufficient \emph{and necessary} condition for the positive definiteness of the global Hessian matrix:

\begin{theorem}[Hessian positive definiteness criterion ({\cite[Thm.~4.12]{ferraroParallelIterativeMethod2025}})]\label{thm:hessian-pd-criterion}
	The Hessian matrix $H(\mathbf{q})$ is positive definite if and only if the matrices defined iteratively by $\Lambda_1 = \mathcal{D}_1$ and
	\[ \Lambda_i = \mathcal{D}_i - \mathcal{C}_{i-1}^T \Lambda_{i-1}^{-1} \mathcal{C}_{i-1}, \quad 2 \leq i \leq N - 1 \]
	are all positive definite.
\end{theorem}
This check has a runtime complexity of $\mathcal{O}(N n^3)$, which is the same in terms of $N$ as \cref{thm:local-hessian-pd-convergence}, except that this check is a bi-implication.
