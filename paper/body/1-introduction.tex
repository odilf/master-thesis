\chapter{Introduction}

In the Lagrangian formulation of mechanics, possible trajectories of a system in a configuration space ($q(t) \in Q$, where $Q$ is a smooth manifold) are given as the stationary points of the action, defined as $S(q(\cdot)) =\int_0^T L(q(t), \dot{q}(t)) \odif{t}$. While it is convenient to work analytically with these equations when possible, in practice the Lagrangian is often either too complicated to handle this way or fundamentally numerical. These cases are intractable to handle analytically, so it is very common to instead find approximations numerically.

In this thesis we give an overview of discretization techniques used to find solutions to Lagrangian systems. Namely, we discuss variational integration, where one discretizes the configuration space and the variational principle itself rather than the equations of motion, which preserves geometric properties of the underlying manifold of the system. We also showcase a state-of-the-art algorithm that can solve regular Lagrangian systems on vector spaces in parallel with fixed boundary conditions~\cite{ferraroParallelIterativeMethod2025}.

Building on this algorithm, we extend it to work with forced systems and systems defined on Lie groups as configuration spaces. In each case the extension is largely a matter of adapting the existing derivation to the new structure, but it requires establishing the conditions under which the iterative scheme still converges, which do not carry over automatically. This extension makes the algorithm applicable to many more real-life classes of problems. For instance, forced systems give access to dissipation, nonholonomic constraints and externally controlled forces; and Lie groups to rigid body and multi-body dynamics, such as spacecraft attitude control and robotic manipulators.

While we do not provide a foundational and in-depth reference of mathematical and numerical computations of physical systems, this thesis is meant to serve as a motivated introduction to numerical computations of Euler-Lagrange equations. For instance, a programmer with some mathematical background working on a satellite docking system would be able to implement a solver, but could also understand \emph{why} the algorithm works, and even why it has the structure it has in the first place. We believe that having this insight is valuable, especially as mechanical tasks keep getting more automated.

\paragraph{Objectives} The objectives of this thesis are:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item to review variational integration and the parallel iterative solver for the discrete Euler-Lagrange equations of~\cite{ferraroParallelIterativeMethod2025};
	\item to extend this solver to forced (Lagrange-d'Alembert) systems, together with provable convergence criteria; and
	\item to extend it to configuration spaces that are Lie groups, by trivializing to the Lie algebra, and providing convergence criteria.
\end{enumerate}

Each extension is accompanied by a reference implementation and numerical examples (\cref{sec:forced-examples,sec:lie-examples}) with the source code publicly available at \repo{}.

\paragraph{Structure} The thesis is structured as follows: In \cref{sec:background} we discuss mathematical tools and physical principles that frame the rest of the work. In \cref{sec:euler-lagrange-solutions} we explain how to approach discretization and to compute solutions of Lagrangian systems, showcasing specifically the parallel algorithm as presented in~\cite{ferraroParallelIterativeMethod2025}. Finally, we explain how to generalize said algorithm to forced systems in \cref{sec:forced-systems} and to Lie groups in \cref{sec:lie-groups}; and give conclusions and avenues for future work in \cref{sec:conclusion}.
