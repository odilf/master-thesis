\notion{Smooth manifolds}\label{notion:manifold}~\cite[Chapter 1]{leeSmoothManifolds2013} An $n$-dimensional manifold is a set $Q$ with some topology where for every point $q \in Q$ there exists a neighborhood $U \subset Q$ and a homeomorphism $\phi : U \to \hat{U}$ where $\hat{U} \subset \RR^n$. The tuple $(U, \phi)$ is called a \newterm{chart}, and can be thought of as providing \newterm{local coordinates} around a point $q$.

Multiple charts can form an \newterm{Atlas} if the neighborhoods $U_i$ cover the original set $V$. Two charts $U_i$ and $U_j$ are \newterm{smoothly compatible} if $\phi_i \circ \phi_j^{-1}$ is smooth on the intersection $U_i \cap U_j$ (where by ``smooth'' we mean $C^\infty$ \todo{Is this necessary to clarify?}). A manifold with an atlas composed of smoothly compatible charts is a \newterm{smooth manifold} \todo{Technically we're missing maximally smooth atlasses\ldots}.

On a more intuitive level, smooth manifolds are topological spaces that look locally like $\RR^n$ and where doing a change of coordinates (or a change of ``basis'') is smooth.

\notion{Tangent spaces}~\cite[Chapter 3]{leeSmoothManifolds2013} The tangent space at point $q \in Q$ is denotes as $T_q Q$ and is defined by representatives of:
\[ T_q Q = \{ \dot{\gamma}(0) \, | \, \gamma(0) = q \} \]
where $\gamma(t) : (-\varepsilon, \varepsilon) \to Q$ are differentiable paths on $Q$. Tangent spaces always form $n$-dimensional vector spaces. These vector spaces have duals, called \newterm{cotangent spaces} $T_q^* Q$ that can be interpreted as linear forms $T_q Q \to Q$.

The \newterm{tangent bundle} is the disjoint union of all tangent spaces, $TQ = \sqcup_q T_q Q$, which forms a $2n$-dimensional manifold itself, which is however not in general a vector space, unless the original manifold is one too. The \newterm{cotangent bundle} is defined in the same way.

For our purposes, a mapping on tangent and cotangent bundles $f : TM \to TM$ (or $f : T^*M \to T^*M$, or any combination thereof \todo{$f : TM \text{ or } T^*M \to TM \text{ or } T^*M$?}) is said to be \newterm{fiber-preserving} if for every $q \in M$ and every $\eta \in T_q M$, $f(\eta) \in T_q M$. In other words, $f$ does not move the point at which it is tangent \todo{Maybe remove here and just mention in forces.}.

\notion{Differential}~\cite[p. 55]{leeSmoothManifolds2013} A differential or derivative of a function on manifolds $f : M \to N$ evaluated at $p \in M$ is a mapping between the tangent spaces of the manifolds $T_p f : T_p M \to T_{f(p)} N$, defined as $T_p f (\dot{\gamma}(0)) = \odv{}{t} \big|_0 f(\gamma(t))$.

\notion{Lie groups}~\cite[Chapter 7]{leeSmoothManifolds2013} A Lie group is a smooth manifold $G$ equipped with a group operation $\cdot : G \times G \to G$ respecting the usual group axioms. The tangent space at the identity of a Lie group is called a \newterm{Lie algebra}, denoted by $\fg$ (called this way because it forms an algebra with the Lie bracket, although we won't use it in this paper). We define the left and right application operators as $L_g = h \mapsto g \cdot h$ and $R_g = h \mapsto h \cdot g$ respectively.

\notion{Lie algebra trivialization}\label{notion:lie-algebra-trivialization} The group structure of a Lie group allows us to \newterm{trivialize} tangent spaces to the Lie algebra. Specifically, the entire tangent bundle $TQ$ is isomorphic to $G \times \fg$ via the differential of the left-application, $T_e L_g : \fg \to T_g G$. In other words, every tangent vector on a Lie group can be described by the point at which it is tangent, and an element of $\fg$.

\notion{Differential forms*}\label{notion:differential-forms}~\cite[Chapter 14]{leeSmoothManifolds2013} A \newterm{1-form} on a manifold $M$ is a smooth assignment of a covector $\alpha_p \in T_p^* M$ to each $p \in M$ (i.e., a covector field). The differential of a function $Q \to \RR$ is an example of a one-form.

More generally, a $k$-\newterm{form} on $M$ is a smooth assignment of an alternating multilinear map $\omega_p : T_p M \times \cdots \times T_p M \to \RR$ with $k$ arguments for each point $p \in M$. ``Alternating'' means that swapping any two arguments flips the sign. In particular, a 2-form satisfies $\omega(v, w) = - \omega(w, v)$. A $k$-form is often interpreted as providing a signed measure of the infinitesimal parallelepiped spanned by $k$ tangent vectors, with a possibly position-dependent weight.

\notion{Wedge product*} The \newterm{wedge product} of two 1-forms $\alpha$ and $\beta$ is the 2-form
\[ (\alpha \wedge \beta)(v, w) = \alpha(v) \beta(w) - \alpha(w) \beta(v). \]
In coordinates on $\RR^2$, $\odif{x} \wedge \odif{y}$ evaluated on two tangent vectors returns the signed area of the parallelogram they span (i.e., the determinant). The definition extends to higher degree forms as $\alpha \wedge \beta = {(-1)}^{k l} \beta \wedge \alpha$ for a $k$-form and an $l$-form.

\notion{Pullback of forms*}\label{notion:pullback}~\cite[p. 359]{leeSmoothManifolds2013} Given a smooth map $f : M \to N$ and a $k$-form $\omega$ on $N$, the \newterm{pullback} $f^* \omega$ is the $k$-form on $M$ defined by
\[ {(f^* \omega)}_p (v_1, \ldots, v_k) = \omega_{f(p)}\bigl(T_p f(v_1), \ldots, T_p f(v_k)\bigr). \]
That is, the pullback ``reverses'' the order of operation by pre-applying the differential of $f$, and then evaluating $\omega$ at $f(p)$. Pullbacks respect the wedge product in the sense that $f^* (\alpha \wedge \beta) = f^* \alpha \wedge f^* \beta$. 

\notion{Exterior derivative*}~\cite[Chapter 14]{leeSmoothManifolds2013} The \newterm{exterior derivative} $\textrm{d}: \Omega^k(M) \to \Omega^{k+1}(M)$ is the unique linear operation that agrees with the ordinary differential on functions ($k = 0$), satisfies the graded Leibniz rule
\[ \odif{(\alpha \wedge \beta)} = \odif{\alpha} \wedge \beta + {(-1)}^k \alpha \wedge \odif{\beta} \quad \text{for $\alpha$ a $k$-form,} \]
and squares to zero, $\odif*{}^2 = 0$. A form $\omega$ with $\odif{\omega} = 0$ is called \newterm{closed}.

In coordinates, if $\omega = f \, \odif{x^{i_1}} \wedge \cdots \wedge \odif{x^{i_k}}$ then $\odif{\omega} = \odif{f} \wedge \odif{x^{i_1}} \wedge \cdots \wedge \odif{x^{i_k}}$. For $\RR^3$, applied to a 0-form ($Q \to \RR$) it gives the gradient $\nabla$, to a 1-form the curl $\nabla \times$, and to a 2-form the divergence $\nabla \cdot$. The familiar identities $\nabla \times \nabla = 0$ and $\nabla \cdot (\nabla \times) = 0$ are both instances of $\odif*{}^2 = 0$. 

\notion{Symplectic forms*}\label{notion:symplectic-forms}~\cite[Chapter 22]{leeSmoothManifolds2013} A \newterm{symplectic form} on a manifold $M$ is a 2-form $\omega$ that is closed ($\odif{\omega} = 0$) and non-degenerate, meaning that $\omega_p(v, \cdot) = 0$ as a 1-form implies $v = 0$. Non-degeneracy forces $M$ to be even-dimensional. A diffeomorphism $F : M \to M$ is called \newterm{symplectic} if $F^* \omega = \omega$ for all symplectic forms $\omega$.

\section{Physical systems, configuration space and Lagrangians}\label{sec:physical-systems-lagrangians}

A physical system can be framed using either Hamiltonian or Lagrangian mechanics. In this paper, we focus on the Lagrangian approach, where physical states are elements of some \newterm{configuration space} $Q$, which has to be a smooth manifold; and a \newterm{Lagrangian} $L: TQ \to \RR$.

\paragraph{Configuration space}
 Even though essentially all physical models try to model reality, which ultimately lives in $\RR^3$, the state space need not be $\RR^3$ or even a vector space at all. Using manifolds that have some geometric structure can encode constraints directly in the description of the system, making it impossible by construction to have physically unrealizable states. That is, the geometry of the manifold can encode constraints about periodicity, topology or other types of global constraints.

As a simple example, one can try to encode the state of a pendumul with $\RR^1$, using the angle as a real number. However, this representation does not take into account the periodicity of the angle, since $0$ and $2\pi$ are different elements of $\RR$, but they represent the same physical state. Mathematically, the more accurate approach is to model the angle as an element of $S^1$, which is a smooth manifold that encodes the periodicity, and also reveals facts such as the configuration space being compact, which $\RR$ is not. Using $\RR^1$ means that you have to interpret the fact that $0$ and $2\pi$ are the same state outside of the mathematical framework, where the mathematical rigor can no longer accompany you. Furthermore, if some result hinges on the periodicity of the pendulum, this again needs to be done ``out of band'' and it cannot be rigorously, mathematically, justified anymore.

While these problems might seem minor for the pendulum example, they apply similarly to much more complicated systems. For instance, it might be intuitive to model rotations in $\RR^3$ using Euler angles (i.e., pitch, yaw and roll), but this

\paragraph{Lagrangian}
On the other hand, the Lagrangian encodes the laws of the physical system by governing how states in $Q$ evolve. Namely, we can talk about \newterm{paths} in configuration space $q(t): [0, T] \to Q \in C^2([0, T], Q)$ which define the \newterm{path space of $Q$}, $\mathcal{C}(Q) = C^2([0, T], Q)$. Not all paths are realizable in the physical system, and in fact a path can only occur if it extremizes the \newterm{action} $\action: C(Q) \to \RR$, defined as:

\begin{equation}
	\action(q) = \int_0^T L(q(t), \dot{q}(t)) \odif{t}.
	\label{eq:action}
\end{equation}

This is the fundamental idea that underpins every result in this paper, called \newterm{Hamilton's principle}:

\begin{definition}[Hamilton's principle]\label{def:hamiltons-principle}
	A path $q(t): [0, T] \to Q \in C^2([0, T], Q)$ is the physical trajectory of the Lagrangian system defined by $L : TQ \rightarrow \RR$ if and only if $q$ is a critical point of the functional action $\action$
\end{definition}

The extension to time-dependent Lagrangians, higher-order Lagrangians follow the same Hamilton's principle but with different preservation properties.

We can define a Lagrangian as $L(t, q, \dot{q}): \RR \times TQ \to \RR$, where the first argument is interpreted as a time parameter. This can be thought of as a system where the effective laws of physics change over time. In the example of the heat radiating box, as time advances there is a loss of energy.

Following Hamilton's principle, we seek to find the extrema of the action defined by a Lagrangian $L$. This can be done by taking a variation of the action and equating it to $0$. Namely, we consider paths $q(t) + \delta q(t)$, where $\delta q(0) = \delta q(T) = 0$. Then, we define the variation of the action $\delta S(q)$ as the linear term of $S(q + \delta q)$ with respect to $\delta q$. Applying this to the action, we get:

\begin{align*}
	\delta S(q)                     & = \delta \int_0^T L(q(t), \dot{q}(t)) \odif{t}                                                                                                     \\
	                                & = \int_0^T \left(\pdv{L}{q} \delta q + \pdv{L}{\dot{q}} \delta \dot{q}\right) \odif{t}                                                             \\
	\text{\faint{(by parts)}} \quad & = \int_0^T \left(\pdv{L}{q} \delta q - \odv{}{t} \pdv{L}{\dot{q}} \delta q \right) \odif{t} + \pdv{L}{\dot{q}} \underbrace{\delta q \big|_0^T}_{0} \\
	                                & = \int_0^T \left(\pdv{L}{q} - \odv{}{t} \pdv{L}{\dot{q}} \right) \delta q \, \odif{t}                                                              \\
\end{align*}
and for this to hold given any variation $\delta q$, we need the integrand to be identically $0$. This is how we derive the Euler-Lagrange equation:

\begin{equation}
	\pdv{L}{q} - \odv{}{t} \pdv{L}{\dot{q}} = 0
	\label{eq:euler-lagrange}
\end{equation}

Computing the relevant derivatives of the Lagrangian provides us with a second order differential equation with respect to $q(t)$. A path that satisfies this differential equation is a path that can occur in the system. The ODE arising from a Lagrangian system results in what are often called the \newterm{equations of motion} for that system.
