\section{Basic mathematical notions}\label{sec:mathematical-tools}

As mentioned above, this section is not meant to be an introduction to the topics, but rather more of a clarification/refresher. For each mathematical notion listed, specific references to \cite{leeSmoothManifolds2013} are given, which contain motivated introductions and more in-depth explanations.

Notions~\ref{notion:manifold}--\ref{notion:lie-algebra-trivialization} are used often throughout the paper. Notions~\ref{notion:differential-forms}--\ref{notion:symplectic-forms}, which are annotated with a star, are only invoked when describing certain geometric properties in \cref{sec:geometric-properties-el,sec:pitfalls-discrete-el-ode}, and can be glossed over without too much concern.


\notion{Notation for derivatives}

We use $\mdif{i} f$ to represent the partial derivative of a function $f$ over its $i$-th argument, and interpret repeated indices as higher derivatives, $\mdif{ij} = \mdif{j} \circ \mdif{i}$ (so $\mdif{12} \Ld$ is a mixed second derivative). Without a subscript, $\mdif* f$ denotes the full derivative over all arguments at once. When we differentiate over a named variable instead of a positional argument, we write $\partial_x f$, such as $\partial_{q_k} S_d$; and in the classical continuous setting we also use the Leibniz form $\pdv{L}{q}$. Furthermore, $\odv{f}{t}$ is the derivative over a scalar, $T_p f$ the differential of a map between manifolds (\ref{notion:differential}), and $\odif{\alpha}$ the exterior derivative (\ref{notion:exterior-derivative}).

\notion{Smooth manifolds}\label{notion:manifold}~\cite[\S1]{leeSmoothManifolds2013} An $n$-dimensional manifold is a set $Q$ with some topology where for every point $q \in Q$ there exists a neighborhood $U \subset Q$ and a homeomorphism $\phi : U \to \hat{U}$ where $\hat{U} \subset \RR^n$. The tuple $(U, \phi)$ is called a \newterm{chart}, and can be thought of as providing \newterm{local coordinates} around a point $q$.

Multiple charts can form an \newterm{atlas} if the neighborhoods $U_i$ cover the original set $Q$. Two charts $(U_i, \phi_i)$ and $(U_j, \phi_j)$ are \newterm{smoothly compatible} if $\phi_i \circ \phi_j^{-1}$ is smooth on the intersection $U_i \cap U_j$ (where by ``smooth'' we mean $C^\infty$). A manifold with an atlas composed of smoothly compatible charts is a \newterm{smooth manifold} (see also \cite{marsdenIntroductionMechanicsSymmetry1999} for more details)%\todo{Technically we're missing maximally smooth atlas\ldots}.

On a more intuitive level, smooth manifolds are topological spaces that look locally like $\RR^n$ and where doing a change of coordinates (or a change of ``basis'') is smooth.

%\todo{Technically manifolds can be on fields other than $\RR$, right?}

\notion{Tangent spaces}~\cite[\S3]{leeSmoothManifolds2013} The tangent space at point $q \in Q$ is denoted as $T_q Q$ and is defined by representatives of:
\[ T_q Q = \{ \dot{\gamma}(0) \, | \, \gamma(0) = q \} \]
where $\gamma(t) : (-\varepsilon, \varepsilon) \to Q$ are differentiable paths on $Q$. Tangent spaces always form $n$-dimensional vector spaces and we denote by $\tau_Q: TQ\rightarrow Q$ the canonical projection. These vector spaces have duals, called \newterm{cotangent spaces} $T_q^* Q$ that can be interpreted as linear forms $T_q Q \to \RR$. Also, $\pi_Q: T^*Q\rightarrow Q$ denotes the corresponding canonical projection. 

The \newterm{tangent bundle} is the disjoint union of all tangent spaces, $TQ = \sqcup_q T_q Q$, which forms a $2n$-dimensional manifold itself (that is not in general a vector space). The \newterm{cotangent bundle} is defined in the same way.

For our purposes, a mapping to and from tangent and cotangent bundles such as $f : TM \to TM$ is said to be \newterm{fibre-preserving} if for every $q \in M$ and every $\eta \in T_q M$, $f(\eta) \in T_q M$. In other words, $f$ does not move the point at which it is tangent or $\tau_Q\circ f=\tau_Q$. %\todo{Maybe remove here and just mention in forces.}.

\notion{Differential}\label{notion:differential}~\cite[p.\ 55]{leeSmoothManifolds2013} A differential or derivative of a function on manifolds $f : M \to N$ evaluated at $p \in M$ is a mapping between the tangent spaces of the manifolds $T_p f : T_p M \to T_{f(p)} N$, defined  as $T_p f (\dot{\gamma}(0)) = \odv{}{t}_0 f(\gamma(t))$.

\notion{Lie groups}~\cite[\S7]{leeSmoothManifolds2013} A Lie group is a smooth manifold $G$ equipped with a smooth group operation $\cdot : G \times G \to G$ respecting the usual group axioms (including having a smooth inverse). The tangent space at the identity of a Lie group is called a \newterm{Lie algebra}, denoted by $\fg$ (called this way because it forms an algebra with the Lie bracket, although we will not use it in this paper). We define the left and right application operators as $L_g = h \mapsto g \cdot h$ and $R_g = h \mapsto h \cdot g$ respectively.

\notion{Lie algebra trivialization}\label{notion:lie-algebra-trivialization} The group structure of a Lie group allows us to \newterm{trivialize} tangent spaces to the Lie algebra. Specifically, the entire tangent bundle $TG$ is isomorphic to $G \times \fg$ via the differential of the left-application, $T_e L_g : \fg \to T_g G$. In other words, every tangent vector on a Lie group can be described by the point at which it is tangent, and an element of $\fg$.

\notion{Differential forms*}\label{notion:differential-forms}~\cite[\S14]{leeSmoothManifolds2013} A \newterm{1-form} on a manifold $M$ is a smooth assignment of a covector $\alpha_p \in T_p^* M$ to each $p \in M$ (i.e., a covector field). The differential of a function $Q \to \RR$ is an example of a one-form.

More generally, a $k$-\newterm{form} on $M$ is a smooth assignment of an alternating multilinear map $\omega_p : T_p M \times \cdots \times T_p M \to \RR$ with $k$ arguments for each point $p \in M$. ``Alternating'' means that swapping any two arguments flips the sign. In particular, a 2-form satisfies $\omega(v, w) = - \omega(w, v)$. A $k$-form is often interpreted as providing a signed measure of the infinitesimal parallelepiped spanned by $k$ tangent vectors, with a possibly position-dependent weight.

\notion{Wedge product*} The \newterm{wedge product} of two 1-forms $\alpha$ and $\beta$ is the 2-form
\[ (\alpha \wedge \beta)(v, w) = \alpha(v) \beta(w) - \alpha(w) \beta(v). \]
In coordinates on $\RR^2$, $\odif{x} \wedge \odif{y}$ evaluated on two tangent vectors returns the signed area of the parallelogram they span (i.e., the determinant). The definition extends to higher degree forms as $\alpha \wedge \beta = {(-1)}^{k l} \beta \wedge \alpha$ for a $k$-form and an $l$-form.

\notion{Pullback of forms*}\label{notion:pullback}~\cite[p. 359]{leeSmoothManifolds2013} Given a smooth map $f : M \to N$ and a $k$-form $\omega$ on $N$, the \newterm{pullback} $f^* \omega$ is the $k$-form on $M$ defined by
\[ {(f^* \omega)}_p (v_1, \ldots, v_k) = \omega_{f(p)}\bigl(T_p f(v_1), \ldots, T_p f(v_k)\bigr). \]
That is, the pullback ``reverses'' the order of operation by pre-applying the differential of $f$, and then evaluating $\omega$ at $f(p)$. Pullbacks respect the wedge product in the sense that $f^* (\alpha \wedge \beta) = f^* \alpha \wedge f^* \beta$.

\notion{Exterior derivative*}\label{notion:exterior-derivative}~\cite[\S14]{leeSmoothManifolds2013} The \newterm{exterior derivative} $\odif*{}: \Omega^k(M) \to \Omega^{k+1}(M)$ is the unique linear operation that agrees with the ordinary differential on functions ($k = 0$), satisfies the graded Leibniz rule
\[ \odif{(\alpha \wedge \beta)} = \odif{\alpha} \wedge \beta + {(-1)}^k \alpha \wedge \odif{\beta} \quad \text{for $\alpha$ a $k$-form,} \]
and squares to zero, $\odif*{}^2 = 0$. A form $\omega$ with $\odif{\omega} = 0$ is called \newterm{closed}.

In coordinates, given $f : U \to \RR$, if $\omega = f \, \odif{x^{i_1}} \wedge \cdots \wedge \odif{x^{i_k}}$ then $\odif{\omega} = \odif{f} \wedge \odif{x^{i_1}} \wedge \cdots \wedge \odif{x^{i_k}}$. For $\RR^3$, applied to a 0-form ($Q \to \RR$) it gives the gradient $\nabla$, to a 1-form the curl $\nabla \times$, and to a 2-form the divergence $\nabla \cdot$. The identities $\nabla \times \nabla = 0$ and $\nabla \cdot (\nabla \times) = 0$ are both instances of $\odif*{}^2 = 0$.

\notion{Symplectic forms*}\label{notion:symplectic-forms}~\cite[\S22]{leeSmoothManifolds2013} A \newterm{symplectic form} on a manifold $M$ is a 2-form $\omega$ that is closed ($\odif{\omega} = 0$) and non-degenerate, meaning that $\omega_p(v, \cdot) = 0$ as a 1-form implies $v = 0$. Non-degeneracy forces $M$ to be even-dimensional. A diffeomorphism $F : M \to M$ is called \newterm{symplectic} it preserves a symplectic form $\omega$, in the sense that $F^* \omega = \omega$.

\section{Physical systems, configuration space and Lagrangians}\label{sec:physical-systems-lagrangians}

A physical system can be framed using either Hamiltonian or Lagrangian mechanics. In this paper, we focus on the Lagrangian approach, where physical states are elements of some \newterm{configuration space} $Q$, which has to be a smooth manifold; and a \newterm{Lagrangian} $L: TQ \to \RR$.

\paragraph{Configuration space}
Even though essentially all physical models try to model reality, which ultimately lives in $\RR^3$, the state space need not be $\RR^3$ or even a vector space at all. Using manifolds that have some geometric structure can encode constraints directly in the description of the system, making it impossible by construction to have physically unrealizable states, like rigid motions. That is, the geometry of the manifold can encode constraints about periodicity, topology or other types of global constraints.

For instance, one can try to encode the state of a pendulum with $\RR^1$, using the angle as a real number. However, this representation does not take into account the periodicity of the angle, since $0$ and $2\pi$ are different elements of $\RR$, but they represent the same physical state. Mathematically, the more accurate approach is to model the angle as an element of $S^1$, which is a smooth manifold that encodes the periodicity. The use of $\RR^1$ forces one to interpret the fact that $0$ and $2\pi$ are the same state outside of the mathematical framework, where mathematical rigor can no longer help. Furthermore, if some result hinges on the periodicity of the pendulum, this again needs to be done ``out of band'' and it cannot be rigorously, mathematically, justified anymore.

While these problems might seem minor for the pendulum example, they apply similarly to much more complicated systems. As a slightly more involved example, it might be intuitive to model rotations in $\RR^3$ using Euler angles (i.e., pitch, yaw and roll), but this leads to gimbal lock and, perhaps more crucially, composition of rotations does not correspond with vector addition, so often most of the work is spent in switching coordinates~\cite[p. 39]{marsdenDiscreteMechanicsVariational2001}. These problems arise from the fundamental fact that 3-dimensional rotations do not have a vector space structure! Instead, we can use $\SO(3)$, the Lie group which correctly encodes these rotations. Statements about rotations on $\SO(3)$ are rigorous and unambiguous.

\newcommand{\action}{S}

\paragraph{Lagrangian}
On the other hand, the Lagrangian encodes the \emph{laws} of the physical system by governing how states in $Q$ evolve. Namely, we can talk about \newterm{paths} in configuration space $q(t): [0, T] \to Q \in C^2([0, T], Q)$ which define the \newterm{path space of $Q$}, $\mathrm{Path}(Q) = C^2([0, T], Q)$. Not all paths are realizable in the physical system, and in fact a path can only occur if it extremizes the \newterm{action} $\action: C^2([0, T], Q)\to \RR$, defined as:

\begin{equation}
	\action(q) = \int_0^T L(q(t), \dot{q}(t)) \odif{t}.
	\label{eq:action}
\end{equation}

This is the fundamental idea that underpins every result in this paper, called \newterm{Hamilton's principle}:

\begin{definition}[Hamilton's principle]\label{def:hamiltons-principle}
	A path $q: [0, T] \to Q \in C^2([0, T], Q)$ is the physical trajectory of the Lagrangian system defined by $L : TQ \rightarrow \RR$ if and only if $q$ is a critical point of the functional action $\action$
\end{definition}

This is the standard Lagrangian formulation, that describes closed systems. In principle this is enough to model any physical system. In practice, however, it is often more convenient to model a smaller subsystem in isolation, even when that subsystem exchanges energy with its surroundings. There are extensions such as time-dependent and higher-order Lagrangians that follow the same Hamilton's principle but with different preservation properties. A few such extensions include:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \newterm{Time-dependent Lagrangians}, $L : \RR \times TQ \to \RR$, depend explicitly on a time parameter. This models a system whose effective laws of physics change over time, typically because it is coupled to an external influence that varies with time. Hamilton's principle applies but energy is no longer conserved.
	\item \newterm{Forced systems} are one of the main topics of this paper (\cref{sec:forced-systems}). They have some forcing applied (such as dissipation) and follow the Lagrange-d'Alembert principle (\cref{def:lagrange-dalembert}).
	\item \newterm{Constrained} systems restrict the admissible configurations or velocities to a submanifold of $TQ$. Classic examples include the rigid links of a robotic arm (holonomic constraints) or rolling contact of a wheel on a surface (nonholonomic constraints).
	\item \newterm{Higher-order} Lagrangians depend on higher derivatives of $q$, e.g. $L(q, \dot{q}, \ddot{q})$. In principle, the results of this paper are able to be carried over automatically to Lagrangians of arbitrary order~\cite{ferraroParallelIterativeMethod2025, gay-balmazInvariantHigherorderVariational2012}.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Euler-Lagrange equations}

Following Hamilton's principle~\cite[\S1.3]{marsdenIntroductionMechanicsSymmetry1999}, we seek to find the extrema of the action defined by a Lagrangian $L$. This can be done by taking a variation of the action and equating it to $0$. Namely, we consider a one-parameter family of paths $q_\varepsilon(t)$ with $q_0 = q$, whose infinitesimal variation is the vector field along $q$ given by $\delta q=\odv*{q_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}_{\varepsilon=0}$, with fixed endpoints $\delta q(0) = \delta q(T) = 0$. We then define the variation of the action as $\delta S(q) = \odv*{S(q_\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon}_{\varepsilon=0}$.
Applying this to the action, we get:

\begin{align}
	\delta S(q)                     & = \delta \int_0^T L(q(t), \dot{q}(t)) \odif{t}                                                                                                     \nonumber           \\
	                                & = \int_0^T \left(\pdv{L}{q} \delta q + \pdv{L}{\dot{q}} \delta \dot{q}\right) \odif{t}                                                             \nonumber           \\
	\text{\faint{(by parts)}} \quad & = \int_0^T \left(\pdv{L}{q} \delta q - \odv{}{t} \pdv{L}{\dot{q}} \delta q \right) \odif{t} + \pdv{L}{\dot{q}} \underbrace{\delta q \big|_0^T}_{0} \label{eq:el-parts} \\
	                                & = \int_0^T \left(\pdv{L}{q} - \odv{}{t} \pdv{L}{\dot{q}} \right) \delta q \, \odif{t}                                                              \nonumber           \\
\end{align}
and for this to hold given any variation $\delta q$, we need the integrand to be identically $0$. This is how we derive the Euler-Lagrange equation:

\begin{equation}
	\pdv{L}{q} - \odv{}{t} \pdv{L}{\dot{q}} = 0
	\label{eq:euler-lagrange}
\end{equation}

Computing the relevant derivatives of the Lagrangian provides us with an implicit second-order differential equation with respect to $q(t)$. A path that satisfies this differential equation is a path that can occur in the system. The ODE arising from a Lagrangian system results in what are often called the \newterm{equations of motion} for that system.
