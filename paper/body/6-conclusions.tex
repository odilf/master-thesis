\chapter{Conclusions and future work}\label{sec:conclusion}

This thesis set out to extend the parallel Jacobi-Newton algorithm of~\cite{ferraroParallelIterativeMethod2025} beyond the unforced vector space setting where it was originally formulated, and carry out the respective convergence analyses. Two extensions were carried out: one to forced (Lagrange-d'Alembert) systems in \cref{sec:forced-systems}, and one to systems whose configuration manifold is a Lie group in \cref{sec:lie-groups}. Sufficient conditions for local convergence (\cref{thm:forced-local-dominance,thm:forced-perturbation,thm:lie-group-convergence}) were found, and the Lie group case inherits the easy-to-compute condition given in~\cref{thm:hessian-pd-criterion}.

The underlying observation of the presented work is that the idea of the parallel algorithm does not fundamentally depend on $Q$ being a vector space, nor on the existence of an action that is being extremized. What it depends on is that the discrete equations of motion be a system of \emph{local} couplings between adjacent nodes, so that the Jacobian of the residual is block-tridiagonal. This locality is derived from the variational discretization (writing the action as a sum $\sum_k \Ld(q_k, q_{k+1})$ over edges) and is preserved by both forcing and by left-trivialization to the Lie algebra. Through this lens, the role played by the vector space hypothesis and by the variational hypothesis becomes clear, and the two extensions can be read as decoupling the algorithm from each in turn.

\Cref{sec:forced-systems} drops the variational hypothesis. The algorithm is trivially adaptable, but the discrete Lagrange-d'Alembert principle is not, in general, the extremization of any functional, so the residual $\hat R$ is no longer a gradient and the matrix $H^f = \nabla \hat{R}$ is no longer a Hessian. Concretely, this means $H^f$ is not symmetric, and the previously used convergence criteria based on \cref{thm:hessian-pd-convergence} are no longer applicable. What survives is the block-tridiagonal structure of $H^f$, which is what lets us prove the two structural criteria of \cref{sec:forced-systems-convergence}: a local condition (\cref{thm:forced-local-dominance}) that generalizes the unforced local-Hessian criterion, and a perturbation result (\cref{thm:forced-perturbation}) that treats the forced case as a continuation of the unforced one, with an explicit smallness bound on the forces.

\Cref{sec:lie-groups} drops the vector space hypothesis. Here, the algorithm itself has to be adapted, computing updates in the Lie algebra and applying them via retractions $\tau : \fg \to G$. For the convergence analysis, the residual is pulled back to the Lie algebra via left-translation and the lifted action on $\fg^{N-1}$ has a (true) Hessian $\widetilde{H}$ with the same block-tridiagonal structure as $H$. The iteration, read in a Lie algebra chart at the solution, is similar to a block Jacobi-quasi-Newton iteration on a vector space. \Cref{thm:lie-group-convergence} therefore looks identical to the vector space theorem, and \cref{thm:hessian-pd-criterion} applies to $\widetilde{H}$ without modification. The work is in showing that ``read in a chart at the solution'' is the right thing to do (\cref{lem:chart-iteration-jacobian}) and in translating the local Hessian blocks into Lie algebra coordinates (\cref{sec:lie-hessian}); once that is set up, the algebraic content of the proofs is the same.

Taken together, these two extensions cover many of the systems that motivate variational integrators in practice, such as dissipative systems and rigid bodies. The criteria obtained are sufficient but not necessary, and the forced and Lie group cases were treated independently rather than in combination, which are some ways that the presented work can be expanded upon.

\section{Future work}

The contributions of this thesis suggest several natural continuations, which we group into two themes: strengthening the convergence theory and extending the parallel algorithm to even more kinds of Lagrangian systems; closing with a few possible refinements of the underlying parallel algorithm that would be transitively helpful to the extensions presented here.

\subsection*{Convergence theory}

The most immediate missing piece is an analog of \cref{thm:hessian-pd-criterion} for forced systems. I.e., a quick way to check in general if the algorithm will converge for some forced Lagrangian. The difficulty comes from the fact that there is no clear way to adapt \cref{thm:hessian-pd-criterion} to forced systems, since we do not have a ``global'' criterion as being positive definite is for unforced systems. The two most straightforward approaches to solve this are to either prove the local condition (\cref{thm:forced-local-dominance}) globally and find a quick way to compute it there, or to directly find a local condition similar to \cref{thm:forced-local-dominance} that is quicker to compute.

As an alternative approach to the analysis of forced systems in general, it might be possible to apply Galley's idea~\cite{galleyClassicalMechanicsNonconservative2013}~\cite[\S\S4.2.1,~4.3.3]{satomartindealmagroDiscreteMechanicsForced2020}, which reformulates forced systems as unforced, constrained systems in a configuration space of twice the dimension, essentially modeling, for each variable, an ``environment'' counterpart  that absorbs the force. A cursory attempt at applying this idea at a simple dissipative example resulted in conditions that were much stricter than the ones obtained from an equivalent, unforced, time-dependent Lagrangian. However, this method is known to lead to elegant results (see, for instance,~\cite{diegoVariationalOrderForced2018}), and it might be possible to get usable criteria if one is careful with the constraining.

A separate loose end is that forced systems and Lie groups were treated independently in \cref{sec:forced-systems} and \cref{sec:lie-groups}. At an implementation level, the combination is straightforward, but the convergence analysis is not as much. The forcing contributes additional cross terms to the local Hessian, and it is not directly clear whether all curvature of the retraction and its derivatives will dissappear at the points of interest as in the unforced case. However, it seems possible that the results from \cref{sec:forced-systems-convergence} can be adapted to work on Lie groups following a similar line of reasoning, perhaps with some additional work.

\subsection*{Further extensions}

The other direction of future work is on extending the algorithm to work with even more kinds of Lagrangian systems. Probably the main interest is the extension to \emph{constrained systems}, such as the rigid links of a robotic arm (holonomic) or rolling contact of a wheel on a surface (nonholonomic). Variational integrators for such systems are well developed in the serial setting \tc{}, but the interaction between the constraint structure and the parallel Jacobi-Newton update is non-trivial: the residual now lives on the constrained submanifold, and the local Hessian must be projected accordingly.

In terms of configuration space, Lie groups are less restrictive than vector spaces, but the structure could be loosened even more. For instance, one could consider \emph{homogeneous groups} $G/H$, where the symmetries are provided by a group external to the manifold. The sphere, $S^n = \SO(n+1)/\SO(n)$, is an example of such a manifold with physical relevance. Another loosening can be done in the form of \emph{Lie groupoids}, a groupoid $G \rightrightarrows M$ where $G$ and $M$ are manifolds, which also model real physical systems~\cite{weinsteinGroupoidsUnifyingInternal1996}.

Beyond the configuration space, the algorithm could also be of use in \emph{optimal control}, where the forcing of \cref{sec:forced-systems} plays the role of a control input and the DEL equations enter as constraints in an optimization problem~\cite{lewisOptimalControl2012}. Whether the parallel structure carries over to that setting is left as an open question.

\subsection*{Refinements of the parallel algorithm}

Finally, it might also be possible to do orthogonal improvements to the parallel algorithm. An example could be adaptive time-stepping (i.e., using a variable timestep $h$), which by default is known to break symplecticty but it might be able to be mitigated with some modifications. Such improvements could, then, be applied to the extensions, either automatically or with some additional work, depending on the nature of the improvement.
