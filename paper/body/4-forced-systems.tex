\chapter{Forced systems}\label{sec:forced-systems}

Lagrangian systems can be extended to \newterm{Lagrangian forced systems}, defined with a \newterm{Lagrangian external force} which is usually defined as a map $f^L : TQ \to T^* Q$~\cite[\S4.1.1]{satomartindealmagroDiscreteMechanicsForced2020} that is fibre-preserving. In this setting it is not Hamilton's principle that applies, but rather its forced analog, called the \newterm{Lagrange-d'Alembert principle}:
\begin{definition}[Lagrange-d'Alembert principle {\cite[Def.~4.1.1]{satomartindealmagroDiscreteMechanicsForced2020}}]
	A path $q(t) : [0, T] \to Q \in C^2([0, T], Q)$ is the physical trajectory of the forced Lagrangian system defined by $L: TQ \to \RR$ and force $f^L : TQ \to T^*Q$ if and only if
	\[
		\odv{}{t} \pdv{L}{\dot{q}} - \pdv{L}{q} = f^L (q(t), \dot{q}(t))
	\]
\end{definition}
Unlike Hamilton's principle, this is \emph{not} in general the extremization of any action.

As in the unforced case, it is possible to carry out a discretization of the state space, the Lagrangian and the forces. The first two are the same: from $TQ$ to $Q \times Q$ and from $L : TQ \to \RR$ to $L_d : Q \times Q \to \RR$.

The discretization of the force is similar to the one of the Lagrangian, except that the force $f : TQ \to T^*Q$ is mapped to a \emph{pair} of discrete forces $f^\pm_d : Q \times Q \to T^*Q$ (where $f^-$ is fibre-preserving with respect to the first  argument, that is, $\hbox{pr}_1=\pi_Q \circ f_d^-$; and $f^+$ with the second, $\hbox{pr}_2=\pi_Q \circ f_d^+$)~\cite[\S3.2.1]{marsdenDiscreteMechanicsVariational2001}.

\begin{definition}[Discrete Lagrange-d'Alembert principle {\cite[Def.~4.2.2]{satomartindealmagroDiscreteMechanicsForced2020}}]\label{def:lagrange-dalembert}
	A path $\mathbf{q}$ is physical if and only if, for all virtual displacements ${\{\delta q_k\}}_{k=1}^{N-1}$ with $\delta q_0 = \delta q_N = 0$,
	\begin{equation*}
		\sum_{k=1}^{N-1} \Bigl( \mdif{2} \Ld(q_{k-1}, q_k) + f_d^+(q_{k-1}, q_k) + \mdif{1} \Ld(q_k, q_{k+1}) + f_d^-(q_k, q_{k+1}) \Bigr) \cdot \delta q_k = 0.
	\end{equation*}
\end{definition}

This discretization preserves the relevant geometric properties the same way that is explained in \cref{sec:geometric-properties-el,sec:variational-integration}.
Solutions that satisfy the discrete Lagrange-d'Alembert principle are given by the forced analog of~\eqref{eq:euler-lagrange-discrete}, the \newterm{forced discrete Euler-Lagrange equations}:
\begin{equation}
	\mdif{2} L_d (q_{k-1}, q_k) + f_d^+ (q_{k-1}, q_k) + \mdif{1} L_d (q_k, q_{k+1}) + f_d^- (q_k, q_{k+1}) = 0
	\label{eq:euler-lagrange-discrete-forced}
	\tag{FDEL}
\end{equation}

\section{The parallel algorithm on forced systems}

Extending the parallel algorithm (\cref{sec:parallel-algorithm}) to use these forces comes down to just modifying the expression of the residual by inserting~\eqref{eq:euler-lagrange-discrete-forced} instead of~\eqref{eq:euler-lagrange-discrete}. Hence, the global residual is modified from~\eqref{eq:residual-global} to
\begin{equation*}
	\hat{R}(\mathbf{q}) = \bigoplus_{k=1}^{N-1} \left(
	\mdif{2} L_d (q_{k-1}, q_k) + f_d^+ (q_{k-1}, q_k) + \mdif{1} L_d (q_k, q_{k+1}) + f_d^- (q_k, q_{k+1})\right)
\end{equation*}
and the local residual, from \cref{eq:residual-local} to
\begin{equation*}
	\hat{r}_k(\mathbf{q}, \overline{q}) =
	\mdif{2} L_d (q_{k-1}, \overline{q}) + f_d^+ (q_{k-1}, \overline{q}) + \mdif{1} L_d (\overline{q}, q_{k+1}) + f_d^- (\overline{q}, q_{k+1}).
\end{equation*}

Utilizing these definitions for the residual, and supposing that $Q$ is a vector space, the computation based on the parallel algorithm is the exact same (which is, incidentally, why we introduced the method based on residuals in \cref{sec:parallel-algorithm}). In fact, it is easy to see that the unforced case already known is recovered by setting the forces to $0$ in the forced case.

\section{Convergence}\label{sec:forced-systems-convergence}

Figuring out the convergence criteria is trickier. A natural way to proceed is to try to make an argument similar to how~\cite[Prop. 4.5]{ferraroParallelIterativeMethod2025} is proved. In short, they pose the problem as an extremization $f = \nabla F$, they calculate the Hessian $H = \nabla^2 F$, and if $H$ is p.d.\ then the algorithm converges. However, this approach fundamentally breaks down because, as mentioned previously, the discrete Lagrange-d'Alembert principle is \emph{not} necessarily an extremization of some $F$.

\begin{remark}[Conservative forces]
	It is easy to find convergence criteria for a specific family of forces, called \newterm{conservative forces}, which \emph{can} be written in terms of a \newterm{discrete potential} $V_d : Q \times Q \to \RR$ as:
	\[
		f_d^+(q_k, q_{k+1}) = -\mdif{2} V_d (q_k, q_{k+1})
		\qquad
		f_d^-(q_k, q_{k+1}) = -\mdif{1} V_d(q_k, q_{k+1}).
	\]
	This is because this is precisely the case where the Lagrange-d'Alembert principle \emph{is} an extremization. In fact, any system with Lagrangian $L_d$ with forces that admit a discrete potential $V_d$ is identical to an unforced system with Lagrangian $L'_d = L_d - V_d$. In other words, for conservative forces, every theorem of~\cite{ferraroParallelIterativeMethod2025} applies trivially. In the rest of the discussion we focus on non-conservative forces.
\end{remark}

For generic forces, the relevant criterion that can be applied is given in~\cite[Lem.~4.4]{ferraroParallelIterativeMethod2025} which we restate here:

\begin{lemma}[Convergence of 1-step Jacobi-Newton]\label{lem:jacobi-newton-convergence}
	For a general smooth system $f(x) = 0$ (not necessarily a gradient system), the 1-step block Jacobi-Newton method converges locally to $x^*$ if the diagonal blocks of $\mdif* f(x^*)$ are regular and $\rho(J(x^*)) < 1$, where $J = -D^{-1}C$, and $D$ and $C$ are the diagonal and off-diagonal parts of $\mdif* f$ respectively.
\end{lemma}

In practice, to determine whether a specific system converges, there might be no better method than directly estimating $\rho(J(\mathbf{q}^*))$ by power iteration (i.e., Arnoldi/Lanczos). This requires factoring each diagonal block of $\mdif* f(\mathbf{q}^*)$ once (an $O(N n^3)$ setup), then performing block-tridiagonal matrix-vector products at $O(N n^2)$ per power-iteration step until the dominant eigenvalue stabilizes. If all one wants is a yes/no answer for a concrete problem, this is the most practical method.

In the remainder of this section we aim to provide \emph{structural} sufficient conditions: closed-form criteria stated in terms of the discrete Lagrangian and forces themselves, rather than the assembled iteration matrix. These are valuable for several reasons: they let us prove convergence for entire classes of systems at once (rather than path by path), they generalize the unforced criterion (\cref{thm:local-hessian-pd-convergence}) and make the role of the forces in convergence explicit, and they connect to standard structural conditions in numerical analysis (Gershgorin-style diagonal dominance). With this in mind, we proceed.

\bigskip

To get an expression for $J^f$, we start by computing the derivative of~\eqref{eq:euler-lagrange-discrete-forced} which we will name $H^f$ as it is the forced analog of $H$ in~\cite{ferraroParallelIterativeMethod2025} (even though $H^f$ is not the Hessian of any function). The result is the following (similar to~\eqref{eq:hessian-matrix}):
\begin{align*}
	H^f_{ij} (\mathbf{q})
	 & = \mdif*{\hat{R}(\mathbf{q})}                                                                                                                                \\
	 & = \pdv{\left(\mdif{2} L_d (q_{k-1}, q_k) + f_d^+ (q_{k-1}, q_k) + \mdif{1} L_d (q_k, q_{k+1}) + f_d^- (q_k, q_{k+1})\right)}!{q_k}                           \\
	 & = \delta_{i, j} \left( \mdif{22}{\Ld}(q_{i - 1}, q_i) + \mdif{2}{f_d^+}(q_{i-1}, q_i) + \mdif{11}{\Ld}(q_i, q_{i+1}) + \mdif{1}{f_d^-}(q_i, q_{i+1}) \right) \\
	 & + \delta_{i+1,j} \left(\mdif{12}{\Ld}(q_i, q_{i+1}) + \mdif{2}{f_d^-}(q_i, q_{i+1}) \right)                                                                  \\
	 & + \delta_{i-1,j} \left(\mdif{21}{\Ld}(q_{i-1}, q_i) + \mdif{1}{f_d^+}(q_{i-1}, q_i) \right).
\end{align*}

This again has a tridiagonal structure, but is not (necessarily) symmetric. In~\eqref{eq:hessian-terms-1} we only had to define the diagonal and the off-diagonal matrices, but now we need three matrices
\begin{align*}
	\hat{\mathcal{D}}_k   & = \mdif{22} \Ld(q_{k-1}, q_k) + \mdif{2} f_d^+(q_{k-1}, q_k) + \mdif{11} \Ld(q_k, q_{k+1}) + \mdif{1} f_d^-(q_k, q_{k+1}) \\
	\hat{\mathcal{C}}^+_k & = \mdif{12} \Ld(q_k, q_{k+1}) + \mdif{2} f_d^-(q_k, q_{k+1})                                                              \\
	\hat{\mathcal{C}}^-_k & = \mdif{21} \Ld(q_k, q_{k+1}) + \mdif{1} f_d^+(q_k, q_{k+1})
\end{align*}

The resulting global Hessian matrix is, then:
\begin{equation}
	H^f (\mathbf{q}) = \begin{pmatrix}
		\hat{\mathcal{D}}_1     & \hat{\mathcal{C}}^+_{1} &                             &                             &                             \\
		\hat{\mathcal{C}}^-_{1} & \hat{\mathcal{D}}_2     & \hat{\mathcal{C}}^+_{2}     &                             &                             \\
		                        & \ddots                  & \ddots                      & \ddots                      &                             \\
		                        &                         & \hat{\mathcal{C}}^-_{N - 3} & \hat{\mathcal{D}}_{N - 2}   & \hat{\mathcal{C}}^+_{N - 2} \\
		                        &                         &                             & \hat{\mathcal{C}}^-_{N - 2} & \hat{\mathcal{D}}_{N - 1}
	\end{pmatrix}
	\label{eq:hessian-matrix-forced}
\end{equation}

We also define auxiliary matrices, similar to~\eqref{eq:hessian-terms-2}:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		\hat{\mathcal{A}}_k & = \mdif{11} \Ld(q_k, q_{k+1}) + \mdif{1} f_d^-(q_k, q_{k+1}) \\
		\hat{\mathcal{B}}_k & = \mdif{22} \Ld(q_k, q_{k+1}) + \mdif{2} f_d^+(q_k, q_{k+1}) \\
	\end{aligned}
	\label{eq:hessian-terms-forced-2}
\end{equation}
(where we still have that $\hat{\mathcal{D}}_k = \hat{\mathcal{B}}_{k-1} + \hat{\mathcal{A}}_k$).

There are two ways to proceed from here. On one hand, we can try to find some criteria of the forced system directly that imply convergence; or we can search for some criteria on the type of forces that, when added to a converged unforced Lagrangian, would also converge with these forces. In the next two subsections we develop both approaches respectively.

\subsection{A local condition}\label{sec:forced-local-condition}

A natural way to try to extend a result for symmetric to asymmetric matrices is to split them in their \newterm{symmetric} and \newterm{skew} components. Namely, any matrix $M$ can be written as $M = \Sym(M) + \Skew(M)$, where $\Sym(M) = \tfrac{1}{2}(M + M^T)$ is symmetric and $\Skew(M) = \tfrac{1}{2}(M - M^T)$ is skew.

One might expect that an analog of \cref{thm:hessian-pd-convergence} (that the method converges if $H$ is p.d.) might be that the method converges in the forced case if $\Sym(H^f)$ is p.d.. However, this falls short of giving $\rho(J^f) < 1$ on its own (one of the conditions of \cref{lem:jacobi-newton-convergence}). A minimal counter-example is $M = \left(\begin{smallmatrix}1 & a \\ -a & 1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ with $D = I$, since then $\Sym(M) = I \succ 0$ for every $a$, but $J = I - D^{-1}M$ has $\rho(J) = |a|$, which is unbounded.

At this point, to motivate the result that will be shown shortly, it might be useful to get a better mental model of what each matrix block represents. The update at an interior node $q_j$ is a Newton step on $R_j^f$ as a function of $q_j$, with the current values of the neighbors $q_{j-1}, q_{j+1}$ held as data. The diagonal block $\hat{\mathcal{D}}_j$ captures how the residual at node $j$ depends on $q_j$ alone, while the off-diagonal blocks $\hat{\mathcal{C}}^+_j$ and $\hat{\mathcal{C}}^-_{j-1}$ capture how it depends on the neighbors. Linearizing $R_j^f$ at $\mathbf{q}^*$, the $j$th component of the global residual at the current path reads $\hat{\mathcal{D}}_j e_j + \hat{\mathcal{C}}^-_{j-1} e_{j-1} + \hat{\mathcal{C}}^+_j e_{j+1}$, where $e_i$ denotes the error at node $i$. The Newton step sets this to zero (to first order) and solves for the new error at $j$: the neighbor errors are multiplied forward by $\hat{\mathcal{C}}^\pm$ (data side), and the resulting residual is converted back into a coordinate update at $j$ by inverting $\hat{\mathcal{D}}_j$ (unknown side). The error transfer factor from a neighbor to $j$ is therefore $\hat{\mathcal{D}}_j^{-1}\hat{\mathcal{C}}^\pm$ (which, incidentally, gives an intuition of why $J^f$ is defined as $-D^{-1} C$).

Crucially, when $\hat{\mathcal{D}}_j$ is ``large enough'' to dominate $\hat{\mathcal{C}}^\pm$, this factor has norm $<1$. That is, $\hat{\mathcal{D}}_j^{-1}$ attenuates the incoming error and the global error contracts at every step, which is what $\rho(J^f) < 1$ conveys.

Of course, the question is how to make ``large enough'' precise. An approach that seems like it would work is to ask whether $\lambda_{\min}(\Sym(H^f)) > \|\Skew(H^f)\|_2$ (this confines the eigenvalues of $H^f$ to a $45^\circ$ sector in the right half-plane and is another natural generalization of $M \succ 0$ to a non-symmetric setting). This is provable in the scalar $n = 1$, $N = 3$ case (see \cref{apx:conjecture-scalar}) but open in the general block case. What we can prove is a stronger condition \emph{per edge}: ask the symmetric part of each diagonal sub-block of $H_k^f$ to strictly dominate the corresponding cross sub-block in spectral norm. This way, we arrive at the following theorem.

\begin{theorem}[Local edge dominance implies convergence]\label{thm:forced-local-dominance}
	Suppose that, at $\mathbf{q}^*$, for every edge $k \in \{0, 1, \ldots, N-1\}$,
	\begin{equation}
		\lambda_{\min}(\Sym(\hat{\mathcal{A}}_k)) > \|\hat{\mathcal{C}}^+_k\|_2,
		\qquad
		\lambda_{\min}(\Sym(\hat{\mathcal{B}}_k)) > \|\hat{\mathcal{C}}^-_k\|_2,
		\label{eq:local-edge-dominance}
	\end{equation}
	with the boundary convention $\hat{\mathcal{C}}^+_{N-1} = \hat{\mathcal{C}}^-_0 = 0$ (these blocks do not appear in $H^f$). Then each diagonal block $\hat{\mathcal{D}}_j$ is invertible, $\rho(J^f(\mathbf{q}^*)) < 1$, and the forced Jacobi-Newton method converges locally to $\mathbf{q}^*$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
	Recall that $\hat{\mathcal{D}}_j = \hat{\mathcal{A}}_j + \hat{\mathcal{B}}_{j-1}$. Since taking $\Sym$ is linear, we also have that $\Sym(\hat{\mathcal{D}}_j) = \Sym(\hat{\mathcal{A}}_j) + \Sym(\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{j-1})$.
	By Weyl's inequality and then by hypothesis:
	\begin{align}
		\lambda_{\min}(\Sym(\hat{\mathcal{D}}_j))
		 & \geq \lambda_{\min}(\Sym(\hat{\mathcal{A}}_j)) + \lambda_{\min}(\Sym(\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{j-1})) \\
		 & > \|\hat{\mathcal{C}}^+_j\|_2 + \|\hat{\mathcal{C}}^-_{j-1}\|_2                                \\
		 & \geq 0,
	\end{align}
	so $\Sym(\hat{\mathcal{D}}_j) \succ 0$. Then, for any $v \neq 0$, Cauchy-Schwarz gives $\|\hat{\mathcal{D}}_j v\|_2 \|v\|_2 \geq v^T \hat{\mathcal{D}}_j v = v^T \Sym(\hat{\mathcal{D}}_j) v \geq \lambda_{\min}(\Sym(\hat{\mathcal{D}}_j)) \|v\|_2^2$, hence $\hat{\mathcal{D}}_j$ is invertible with
	\begin{equation}
		\|\hat{\mathcal{D}}_j^{-1}\|_2 \;\leq\; \frac{1}{\lambda_{\min}(\Sym(\hat{\mathcal{D}}_j))}.
		\label{eq:diag-block-inv-bound}
	\end{equation}

	Now, recall that $J^f = -D^{-1}(L+U)$, where $D$ is the block-diagonal of $H^f$ and $L+U$ is its off-diagonal part. Since $H^f$ is block-tridiagonal, so is $L+U$, with $(L+U)_{j,j-1} = \hat{\mathcal{C}}^-_{j-1}$ and $(L+U)_{j,j+1} = \hat{\mathcal{C}}^+_j$. Left-multiplying by the block-diagonal matrix $-D^{-1}$ acts row-block-by-row-block: the $j$th row-block of $D^{-1}(L+U)$ picks up only the $(j,j)$-block of $D^{-1}$, namely $\hat{\mathcal{D}}_j^{-1}$, since every other column of row $j$ of $D^{-1}$ is zero. So the entire $j$th row of $L+U$ is just rescaled on the left by $\hat{\mathcal{D}}_j^{-1}$, and its sparsity pattern is preserved. Reading this off, $J^f$ is block-tridiagonal with $(J^f)_{j,j-1} = -\hat{\mathcal{D}}_j^{-1}\hat{\mathcal{C}}^-_{j-1}$ and $(J^f)_{j,j+1} = -\hat{\mathcal{D}}_j^{-1}\hat{\mathcal{C}}^+_j$:
	\[
		J^f = \begin{pmatrix}
			0                                              & -\hat{\mathcal{D}}_1^{-1}\hat{\mathcal{C}}^+_1 &                                                        &        \\
			-\hat{\mathcal{D}}_2^{-1}\hat{\mathcal{C}}^-_1 & 0                                              & -\hat{\mathcal{D}}_2^{-1}\hat{\mathcal{C}}^+_2         &        \\
			                                               & \ddots                                         & \ddots                                                 & \ddots \\
			                                               &                                                & -\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{N-1}^{-1}\hat{\mathcal{C}}^-_{N-2} & 0
		\end{pmatrix}
	\]
	Since $\rho(J^f) \leq \|J^f\|$ in any induced operator norm, it suffices to bound $\|J^f\|$ in one convenient choice. The block-tridiagonal structure suggests the block max-norm $\|x\|_{\infty,2} = \max_j \|x_j\|_2$, where $x_j \in \RR^n$ is the $j$th size-$n$ block of $x$. Its induced operator norm is
	\[
		\|J^f\|_{\infty,2} \;=\; \sup_{x \neq 0} \frac{\max_j \|(J^f x)_j\|_2}{\|x\|_{\infty,2}}.
	\]

	Therefore, bounding $\|(J^f x)_j\|_2$ by a multiple of $\|x\|_{\infty,2}$ uniformly in $j$ bounds $\rho(J^f)$ by the same constant. Each row of $J^f$ has form
	\[ (J^f x)_j = -\hat{\mathcal{D}}_j^{-1} \hat{\mathcal{C}}^-_{j-1} x_{j-1} - \hat{\mathcal{D}}_j^{-1} \hat{\mathcal{C}}^+_j x_{j+1}, \]
	and submultiplicativity of the spectral norm together with~\eqref{eq:diag-block-inv-bound} give
	\begin{align*}
		\|(J^f x)_j\|_2
		 & \leq \|\hat{\mathcal{D}}_j^{-1} \hat{\mathcal{C}}^-_{j-1}\|_2 \|x_{j-1}\|_2 + \|\hat{\mathcal{D}}_j^{-1} \hat{\mathcal{C}}^+_j\|_2 \|x_{j+1}\|_2                                        \\
		 & \leq \left(\|\hat{\mathcal{D}}_j^{-1} \hat{\mathcal{C}}^-_{j-1}\|_2 + \|\hat{\mathcal{D}}_j^{-1} \hat{\mathcal{C}}^+_j\|_2\right) \|x\|_{\infty, 2}                                     \\
		 & \leq \left(\|\hat{\mathcal{D}}_j^{-1}\|_2\|\hat{\mathcal{C}}^-_{j-1}\|_2 + \|\hat{\mathcal{D}}_j^{-1}\|_2\|\hat{\mathcal{C}}^+_j\|_2\right) \|x\|_{\infty, 2}                           \\
		 & \leq \frac{\|\hat{\mathcal{C}}^-_{j-1}\|_2 + \|\hat{\mathcal{C}}^+_j\|_2}{\lambda_{\min}(\Sym(\hat{\mathcal{A}}_j)) + \lambda_{\min}(\Sym(\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{j-1}))} \|x\|_{\infty, 2}.
	\end{align*}
	By~\eqref{eq:local-edge-dominance} applied at edges $j$ and $j - 1$, the fraction is strictly less than $1$. Taking $\max_j$,
	\[
		\rho(J^f) \;\leq\; \|J^f\|_{\infty, 2} \;\leq\; \max_j \frac{\|\hat{\mathcal{C}}^-_{j-1}\|_2 + \|\hat{\mathcal{C}}^+_j\|_2}{\lambda_{\min}(\Sym(\hat{\mathcal{A}}_j)) + \lambda_{\min}(\Sym(\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{j-1}))} \;<\; 1.
	\]
	Invertibility of every $\hat{\mathcal{D}}_j$ and $\rho(J^f) < 1$ are exactly the hypotheses of~\cref{lem:jacobi-newton-convergence}, which gives local convergence of the forced Jacobi-Newton method to $\mathbf{q}^*$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Reduction to the unforced case]\label{rem:forced-local-recovery}
	When $f_d^\pm = 0$, the forced blocks reduce to
	\begin{align*}
		\hat{\mathcal{A}}_k = \mdif{11}\Ld(q_k, q_{k+1}),\quad
		 & \hat{\mathcal{C}}^+_k = \mdif{12}\Ld(q_k, q_{k+1}),\quad \\
		\hat{\mathcal{B}}_k = \mdif{22}\Ld(q_k, q_{k+1}),\quad
		 & \hat{\mathcal{C}}^-_k = (\hat{\mathcal{C}}^+_k)^T.
	\end{align*}
	All four are symmetric or transposes of each other, so $\Sym(\hat{\mathcal{A}}_k) = \hat{\mathcal{A}}_k$, $\Sym(\hat{\mathcal{B}}_k) = \hat{\mathcal{B}}_k$, and $\|\hat{\mathcal{C}}^+_k\|_2 = \|\hat{\mathcal{C}}^-_k\|_2$. The two inequalities of~\eqref{eq:local-edge-dominance} therefore collapse to the single per-edge condition
	\[
		\min\!\left(\lambda_{\min}(\mdif{11}\Ld),\, \lambda_{\min}(\mdif{22}\Ld)\right) > \|\mdif{12}\Ld\|_2,
	\]
	which is exactly the diagonal-block dominance condition on the unforced local Hessian $H_k$: each diagonal block must dominate the off-diagonal coupling in spectral norm, which in particular implies $H_k \succ 0$ and recovers the convergence criterion of \cref{thm:local-hessian-pd-convergence}.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Time complexity]\label{rem:forced-local-complexity}
	Checking~\eqref{eq:local-edge-dominance} reduces to two smallest\Hyphdash{eigenvalue} computations on $\Sym(\hat{\mathcal{A}}_k)$ and $\Sym(\hat{\mathcal{B}}_k)$, $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$ each, and two spectral-norm computations on $\hat{\mathcal{C}}^+_k$ and $\hat{\mathcal{C}}^-_k$ ($\mathcal{O}(n^3)$ each via SVD, cheaper via power iteration). Total cost is $\mathcal{O}(N n^3)$, of the same order as the unforced check and as the power-iteration estimate of $\rho(J^f)$ discussed at the start of the section.
\end{remark}

\subsection{A perturbation condition}

In this section we obtain a condition on forces such that, if the unforced problem converges, then the forced system will too. The main idea is to decompose the Hessian as
\begin{equation}
	H^f(\mathbf{q}) = H(\mathbf{q}) + \Delta F(\mathbf{q}),
	\label{eq:hessian-matrix-forced-relative}
\end{equation}
where $H$ is the unforced Hessian~\eqref{eq:hessian-matrix} and $\Delta F$ is a block-tridiagonal \newterm{force-perturbation matrix}, with blocks
\begin{align*}
	\Delta\hat{\mathcal{D}}_k   & = \mdif{2} f_d^+(q_{k-1}, q_k) + \mdif{1} f_d^-(q_k, q_{k+1}), \\
	\Delta\hat{\mathcal{C}}^+_k & = \mdif{2} f_d^-(q_k, q_{k+1}),                                \\
	\Delta\hat{\mathcal{C}}^-_k & = \mdif{1} f_d^+(q_k, q_{k+1}).
\end{align*}
Notice that $\Delta F = 0$ when all forces vanish, recovering $H^f = H$, so $\Delta F$ is a measure of the deviation from the unforced problem.

The following theorem states in a rigorous and quantitative manner the intuitive idea that, if the algorithm already converges without forces, then it still converges when forces are added, as long as they are ``weak enough'', which manifest as the derivatives $\mdif{i} f_d^\pm$ that appear in the Jacobian being small ``enough''.

\begin{theorem}[Perturbation condition]\label{thm:forced-perturbation}
	Suppose the unforced Jacobi-Newton method converges at $\mathbf{q}^*$: the diagonal $D$ of $H(\mathbf{q}^*)$ is invertible and $\rho(J(\mathbf{q}^*)) < 1$. Then there exists an $\varepsilon > 0$ such that, whenever $\|\Delta F(\mathbf{q}^*)\|_2 < \varepsilon$, the forced Jacobi-Newton method also converges locally to $\mathbf{q}^*$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
	Let $D, L, U$ denote the block-diagonal, strict block-lower, and strict block-upper parts of the unforced Hessian $H$, and similarly $\Delta D, \Delta L, \Delta U$ for the perturbation $\Delta F$. Then the forced iteration matrix is
	\[
		J^f = -(D + \Delta D)^{-1}\left( (L + U) + (\Delta L + \Delta U) \right),
	\]
	and the unforced one ($\Delta F = 0$) is $J = -D^{-1}(L + U)$ with $\rho(J) < 1$ by hypothesis.

	First, notice that the map $\Delta F \mapsto J^f$ is continuous at $\Delta F = 0$. This is because each $D_k$ is invertible by hypothesis, so $\|D^{-1}\|_2$ is finite block-wise. Since $\Delta D$ is the block-diagonal part of $\Delta F$, the spectral norm satisfies $\|\Delta D\|_2 \leq \|\Delta F\|_2$ (block-diagonal projection does not increase the spectral norm). Hence for $\|\Delta F\|_2 < \|D^{-1}\|_2^{-1}$, we have that $\|\Delta D\|_2 < \|D^{-1}\|_2^{-1}$. Then, we can write the Neumann series (\cite[Corollary 5.6.16]{hornMatrixAnalysis2012})
	\[
		(D + \Delta D)^{-1} = D^{-1}\sum_{n \geq 0} (-\Delta D \, D^{-1})^n
	\]
	which converges uniformly on a neighborhood of $\Delta F = 0$. The series itself is the inverse, establishing that $D + \Delta D$ is invertible there; and since each partial sum is a polynomial (hence, continuous) function of $\Delta D$, uniform convergence implies that $(D + \Delta D)^{-1}$ depends continuously on $\Delta F$. Matrix multiplication and addition are continuous, so $J^f$ also depends continuously on $\Delta F$ on the same neighborhood. In particular, $J^f \to J$ in any matrix norm as $\|\Delta F\| \to 0$.

	Notice also that the spectral radius $\rho$ cannot jump upward under small perturbations of its matrix argument. By Gelfand's formula~\cite[Cor.~5.6.14]{hornMatrixAnalysis2012},
	\[
		\rho(A) = \inf_{k \geq 1} \|A^k\|^{1/k}.
	\]
	Each $A \mapsto \|A^k\|^{1/k}$ is continuous in $A$, and an infimum of continuous functions is upper semicontinuous. Concretely, for any matrix $A_0$ and any $\eta > \rho(A_0)$, there is a neighborhood of $A_0$ (in $M_n(\RR)$) where $\rho(A) < \eta$.


	Combining these two observations, since $\rho(J) < 1$, there is some $\eta$ with $\rho(J) < \eta < 1$. Upper semicontinuity applied at $A_0 = J$ gives a neighborhood $U \subset M_n(\RR)$ of $J$ on which $\rho(A) < \eta$. By continuity of $\Delta F \mapsto J^f$, the preimage $V' = (J^f)^{-1}(U)$ is open in perturbation-space and contains $\Delta F = 0$. Invertibility of $D + \Delta D$ holds on the open set $V'' = \{\|\Delta F\|_2 < \|D^{-1}\|_2^{-1}\}$, also a neighborhood of $\Delta F = 0$. Their intersection $V = V' \cap V''$ is therefore an open neighborhood of $\Delta F = 0$ on which both $J^f \in U$ and $D + \Delta D$ is invertible. Since $V$ is open and contains $0$, it contains an open ball of some radius $\varepsilon > 0$ around $0$, so $\|\Delta F\|_2 < \varepsilon$ implies
	\[
		\rho(J^f) < \eta < 1 \quad\text{and}\quad D + \Delta D \text{ is invertible.}
	\]
	These are exactly the hypotheses of \cref{lem:jacobi-newton-convergence}, which then gives local convergence of the forced Jacobi-Newton method to $\mathbf{q}^*$.

\end{proof}

In~\cref{thm:forced-perturbation} we only prove that such an $\varepsilon$ exists. In the following lemma, we find an explicit computation, under the mild additional assumption that $\|J\| < 1$ holds in some submultiplicative matrix norm (i.e., a norm such that $\|A B\| \leq \|A\| \|B\|$). This condition is stricter than $\rho(J) < 1$, but is typically satisfied for stable variational integrators.

\begin{lemma}[Explicit perturbation bound]\label{lem:forced-perturbation-explicit}
	Assume the hypotheses of \cref{thm:forced-perturbation}, and further that $\|J\| < 1$ in a submultiplicative norm $\|\cdot\|$ in which the block-diagonal projection is contractive (e.g.\ the spectral norm). Then the conclusion of the theorem holds with
	\begin{equation}
		\varepsilon = \frac{1 - \|J\|}{3\,\|D^{-1}\|}.
		\label{eq:forced-perturbation-epsilon}
	\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	Let $\beta = \|D^{-1}\|$ and $\gamma = \|\Delta F\|$. The identity $(D + \Delta D)^{-1} - D^{-1} = -(D + \Delta D)^{-1}\Delta D \, D^{-1}$, combined with $J = -D^{-1}(L+U)$, gives by direct manipulation
	\[
		J^f - J = -(D + \Delta D)^{-1}\left[\Delta D \cdot J + (\Delta L + \Delta U)\right].
	\]
	The three factors on the right are bounded by, respectively, $\|\Delta D\| \leq \gamma$ (projection contractivity), $\|\Delta L + \Delta U\| = \|\Delta F - \Delta D\| \leq 2\gamma$ (triangle inequality), and $\|(D + \Delta D)^{-1}\| \leq \beta/(1 - \beta\gamma)$ (Neumann tail estimate\footnote{We can get this by factoring $D + \Delta D$ as $D(I + D^{-1}\Delta D)$. Then, we known that $|D^{-1}\Delta D| \leq \beta\gamma$ and if $\beta\gamma < 1$, the Neumann series $(I+M)^{-1} = \sum_{k\geq 0}(-M)^k$ converges and so we can take the geometric series of the norms of the Neumann sum, resulting in $|(I + D^{-1}\Delta D)^{-1}| \leq 1/(1-\beta\gamma)$, giving the claimed bound after multiplying by $|D^{-1}| = \beta$.}, valid for $\beta\gamma < 1$). Combining,
	\[
		\|J^f - J\| \leq \frac{\beta\gamma(\|J\| + 2)}{1 - \beta\gamma}.
	\]
	Convergence follows from $\|J^f\| < 1$, which holds if $\|J^f - J\| \leq 1 - \|J\|$. Imposing this on the bound above and solving for $\gamma$:
	\begin{align*}
		\frac{\beta\gamma(\|J\| + 2)}{1 - \beta\gamma} & \leq 1 - \|J\|                  \\
		\beta\gamma(\|J\| + 2)                         & \leq (1-\|J\|)(1 - \beta\gamma) \\
		\beta\gamma\left(\|J\| + 2 + (1-\|J\|)\right)  & \leq 1 - \|J\|                  \\
		3\beta\gamma                                   & \leq 1 - \|J\|                  \\
		\gamma                                         & \leq \frac{1-\|J\|}{3\beta},
	\end{align*}
	results in~\eqref{eq:forced-perturbation-epsilon}.
\end{proof}

We call the factor $1 - \|J\|$ in~\eqref{eq:forced-perturbation-epsilon} the \newterm{contraction margin} of the unforced iteration, and $\|D^{-1}\|$ measures how well-conditioned the diagonal is. A smaller margin or a worse-conditioned diagonal will shrink $\varepsilon$, making the margin of the magnitude of allowed forces tighter.
This perturbation bound is checkable in terms of derivatives of the discrete forces $f_d^\pm$ alone, which depend on the discrete Lagrangian and the chosen discretization of the force, but not on the (unknown) solution trajectory (although it does not say anything about whether the unforced system converges in the first place).

In practice, however, this guarantee is pessimistic, and (somewhat counterintuitively) it weakens as the discretization is refined. For many problems, as $h \to 0$ the contraction margin $1 - \|J\|$ decreases faster than $\|D^{-1}\|$, so $\varepsilon \to 0$ even though the true admissible force grows. This is shown in example~\ref{ex:damped-oscillator}.

\medskip


\begin{remark}[Time complexity]\label{rem:forced-perturbation-complexity}
	Checking the bound~\eqref{eq:forced-perturbation-epsilon} requires, in terms of the path length $N$ and the configuration space dimension $n$:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item $\|D^{-1}\|_2 = \max_k \|\hat{\mathcal{D}}_k^{-1}\|_2$: $N$ independent $n \times n$ block inversions, costing $\mathcal{O}(N n^3)$.
		\item $\|J\|_2$ via power iteration on $J^T J$: $\mathcal{O}(k N n^2)$ for $k$ iterations.
		\item $\|\Delta F\|_2$ at the chosen point: same cost as $\|J\|_2$, $\mathcal{O}(k N n^2)$.
	\end{itemize}
	The dominant cost is therefore $\mathcal{O}(k N n^2)$ (since usually $k \gtrsim n$). This is substantially cheaper than the $\mathcal{O}(N^3 n^3)$ Cholesky or eigendecomposition that would be needed to check positive\Hyphdash{}definiteness of $H^f$ globally.
\end{remark}

\tocless\subsection{Damped harmonic oscillator}\label{ex:damped-oscillator}

Consider a one-dimensional harmonic oscillator with linear viscous damping,
\[
	\ddot{q} + \gamma\dot{q} + \omega^2 q = 0, \qquad \gamma > 0,
\]
which has the following Lagrangian:
\begin{align*}
	L(q,\dot{q})   & = \tfrac{1}{2}\dot{q}^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}\omega^2 q^2 \\
	f^L(q,\dot{q}) & = -\gamma\dot{q}.
\end{align*}
We discretize the Lagrangian and the force with the trapezoidal rule, evaluating both at the two endpoints $q_k, q_{k+1}$ (since the damping is velocity-only, the force quadrature here coincides with the midpoint rule):
\begin{align*}
	L_d(q_k, q_{k+1})   & = \frac{(q_{k+1}-q_k)^2}{2h} - \frac{\omega^2 h}{4}(q_k^2 + q_{k+1}^2) \\
	f_d^+(q_k, q_{k+1}) & = -\frac{\gamma}{2}(q_{k+1}-q_k)                                       \\
	f_d^-(q_k, q_{k+1}) & = -\frac{\gamma}{2}(q_{k+1}-q_k).
\end{align*}
The ``blocks'' of the forced Hessian are just scalars, which by~\eqref{eq:hessian-terms-forced-2} evaluate to
\begin{alignat*}{2}
	\hat{\mathcal{A}}_k   & = \frac{1}{h} - \frac{\omega^2 h}{2} + \frac{\gamma}{2} & \qquad
	\hat{\mathcal{C}}^+_k & = -\frac{1}{h} - \frac{\gamma}{2}                                \\
	\hat{\mathcal{B}}_k   & = \frac{1}{h} - \frac{\omega^2 h}{2} - \frac{\gamma}{2} & \qquad
	\hat{\mathcal{C}}^-_k & = -\frac{1}{h} + \frac{\gamma}{2}.
\end{alignat*}
Comparing with the unforced blocks $\mathcal{A}_k = \mathcal{B}_k = \frac{1}{h} - \frac{\omega^2 h}{2}$ and $\mathcal{C}_k^\pm = -\frac{1}{h}$, the force perturbation matrix $\Delta F$ has
\[
	\Delta\hat{\mathcal{D}}_k = \mdif{2} f_d^+(q_{k-1}, q_k) + \mdif{1} f_d^-(q_k, q_{k+1})
	= -\frac{\gamma}{2} + \frac{\gamma}{2} = 0.
\]
That is, the midpoint quadrature leaves the diagonal blocks of $H^f$ unchanged relative to the unforced system and the damping force enters $H^f$ only through the off-diagonal blocks,
\[
	\Delta\hat{\mathcal{C}}^+_k = -\frac{\gamma}{2}, \qquad \Delta\hat{\mathcal{C}}^-_k = \frac{\gamma}{2}.
\]
Thus $\Delta F$ is purely off-diagonal with antisymmetric blocks ($\Delta\hat{\mathcal{C}}^-_k = -\Delta\hat{\mathcal{C}}^+_k$), so the skew part of $H^f$ grows with $\gamma$ while the symmetric part remains that of the unforced oscillator. Here, $\|\Delta F\|_2$ can be computed exactly. $\Delta F = \tfrac{\gamma}{2} S$, where $S$ is the $(N-1)\times(N-1)$ skew-symmetric tridiagonal Toeplitz matrix with $-1$ on the super-diagonal and $+1$ on the sub-diagonal, which has known eigenvalues $\lambda_j = 2i\cos\tfrac{j\pi}{N}$ for $j = 1, \dots, N-1$. Since $S$ is normal (being skew-symmetric), its singular values are $|\lambda_j|$, so $\|S\|_2 = \max_j|\lambda_j| = 2\cos\tfrac{\pi}{N}$ and
\[
	\|\Delta F\|_2 = \gamma\cos\frac{\pi}{N}.
\]

Assuming the unforced oscillator converges at $\mathbf{q}^*$ for the chosen step size $h$, \cref{thm:forced-perturbation} and \cref{lem:forced-perturbation-explicit} guarantee that the forced method also converges whenever
\[
	\gamma\cos\frac{\pi}{N} < \varepsilon = \frac{1 - \|J^0\|}{3\|D_0^{-1}\|}
	\quad\Rightarrow\quad
	\gamma < \frac{\varepsilon}{\cos(\pi/N)},
\]
where $J^0$ and $D_0^{-1}$ are the Jacobi matrix and inverse diagonal of the unforced system at $\mathbf{q}^*$. This gives a concrete bound on the admissible damping coefficient in terms of the step size and the unforced convergence margin.

\begin{figure}[ht]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{forced-perturbation-bound.png}
	\caption{Guaranteed vs actual convergence threshold for a damped oscillator depending on step size $h = T/N$. The bound $\varepsilon/\cos(\pi/N)$ of \cref{lem:forced-perturbation-explicit} is plotted against the true threshold (the largest $\gamma$ with $\rho(J^f) < 1$, located by bisection). The guarantee is not only conservative, but in fact moves the wrong way under refinement: as $h \to 0$ the admissible $\gamma$ decreases, while the true threshold increases.}\label{fig:forced-perturbation-bound}
\end{figure}

\Cref{fig:forced-perturbation-bound} compares this bound against the true convergence threshold as the step size is refined. We see that the two diverge as $h \to 0$, with the guaranteed $\gamma$ decreasing at order $h$ while the actual threshold grows at order $1/h$ (so the true limit would only be reached if $\gamma h$ was of order one).

The discrepancy has two sources. Firstly, the bound decreases because the numerator scales like $1 - \|J\| = 1 - \cos(\pi/N) \sim \tfrac{1}{2}(\pi/N)^2 \sim h^2$ while the denominator only scales like $\|D^{-1}\| \sim h/2$, giving an overall $\varepsilon \sim h$.

But more fundamentally, the bound is norm-based and cannot see a near-cancellation in how the damping actually moves the spectrum. $H^f$ is Toeplitz and $J^f$ has diagonal $\hat{\mathcal{D}} = 2/h - \omega^2 h$ (independent of $\gamma$), and off-diagonals $-\hat{\mathcal{C}}^\pm/\hat{\mathcal{D}}$, whose eigenvalues are
\[
	\lambda_j = \frac{2\cos(j\pi/N)}{\hat{\mathcal{D}}}\sqrt{\hat{\mathcal{C}}^+\hat{\mathcal{C}}^-}
	= \cos\frac{j\pi}{N}\,\frac{\sqrt{1 - \gamma^2 h^2/4}}{1 - \omega^2 h^2/2},
	\qquad j = 1, \dots, N-1,
\]
so that $\rho(J^f) \approx \cos(\pi/N) \sqrt{1 - \gamma^2 h^2/4}$. The spectral radius depends on the damping only through $\gamma h$ at second order. For $\gamma h < 2$ it even \emph{decreases}, and it only climbs past $1$ on the sign change at $\gamma h = 2$, after which $\rho(J^f) \approx \cos(\pi/N)\sqrt{\gamma^2 h^2/4 - 1}$ grows and crosses $1$ near $\gamma h \approx 2\sqrt{2}$, giving the true threshold growth $\gamma \sim 1/h$. The norm bound, in contrast, must fit a linear penalty $\|J^f - J\| \sim \gamma h$ inside the small margin $1 - \|J\| \sim h^2$, forcing $\gamma \lesssim h$. That is, measured in units of $\gamma h$, the bound has an $O(h^2)$ margin it ``spends against'' while in reality there is an $O(1)$ margin available. This gives an $\sim N^2$ divergence between predicted and actual, which we observe in \cref{fig:forced-perturbation-bound}.


\tocless\subsection{Parachutist in turbulent wind}\label{ex:parachutist}

A parachutist wants to jump from a helicopter at some position $(x_0, y_0)$ and reach a target point on the ground $(x_T, 0)$ at some time $T$ by letting themselves be carried by the wind, which is given as some vector field $W(x, y) = (w_x, w_y)$.

This problem can be modelled with the Lagrangian
\[
	L(x, y, \dot{x}, \dot{y})=\frac{m}{2}(\dot{x}^2+\dot{y}^2)-m g y
\]
where $g$ is the gravitational acceleration and $m$ is the diver's mass. Furthermore, the parachutist feels some drag, which is modelled as an external force:
\begin{align*}
	(F_x, F_y)=(-k(\dot{x}-w_x(x, y)), -k(\dot{y}-w_y(x, y))),
\end{align*}
where $k$ is the parachute drag coefficient.

The problem can be discretized with the midpoint rule, for instance. The only additional data needed are the length of the path $N$, which then gives us the timestep $h = T/N$. Finally, solving it allows us to recover what the initial velocity must be to reach the desired endpoint by just being dragged by the wind.

\end{figure}

It turns out that for this particular wind field that has two vortexes, the parachutist needs to jump with velocity mostly to the right, and slightly upwards.

\Cref{fig:parachutist-many} shows many trajectories from different starting  positions, both for the vortex wind field (\cref{fig:parachutist-many-vortex}) as well as for a wind field that forms a shear (\cref{fig:parachutist-many-shear}). This serves to illustrate a couple non-trivial observations. For instance, trajectory 3 (hexagon) in \cref{fig:parachutist-many-vortex} needs a very high initial velocity, which indicates that it is most likely not realistic for the parachutist to jump from that point and reach the target; and trajectory 2 (star) in \cref{fig:parachutist-many-shear} shows that to reach the endpoint the parachutist has to first jump \emph{away} from the target to reach it in the desired time (i.e., jump up and to the right, while the target is down and to the left).
